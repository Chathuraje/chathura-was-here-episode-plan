## Held questions

1. **C093-I02 reverses the source's intent.** Book 4 (line 177) uses everyday disgust as evidence that the body is intrinsically foul. The card uses the same examples to ask whether disgust follows belief and origin. A reviewer should confirm this is acceptable as a labelled limited analogy, or change the card to held.
2. **C095 "recall the one calm trait" technique** (Book 4 lines 150–156). An enemy whose bodily conduct is calm but whose speech is not: attend only to the calm part. This could strengthen C095-I02 or become a separate card on selective attention toward someone resented. It is not carded now.
3. **C095 gift exchange with an enemy** (Book 4 line 261; the Cittalapabbata elder's bowl). The source says anger "certainly" subsides through giving. This is a vivid mechanism but rests on one exemplum. Check whether a generosity concept elsewhere supports it.
4. **C095 equanimity grounded in ownership of action** (Margaya line 47; Book 4 lines 60, 188–192, 497). C095-I03 excludes this basis. A reviewer should decide whether the card is still faithful enough without it.
5. **C095 goodwill toward the dead yields no absorption** (Book 4 line 90; Margaya line 14). There may be a grief-related question here, but the source's reason is purely meditative, so it is not carded.

## Content that must not be carried into ideas (flag for all downstream agents)

- **C093:** the body as intrinsically foul (a body-image risk). Seeing all people as "heaps of corpse-parts" (Book 4 line 326; Pradeepika line 205) is dehumanising if generalised. Pre-modern physiology includes heart-blood colour by temperament, liver size by wisdom (Book 4 lines 231–237), 32 kinds of stomach worms (line 261), intestine length differing by sex (line 249), and bile causing madness and shamelessness (line 275).
- **C095:** equanimity based on "beings get what their actions earn". It risks blaming people for their suffering or excusing inaction. Other material to exclude:
  - Compassion for enemies because of their future in hell (Book 4 line 160).
  - Compassion example: a mutilated, poor beggar described as "of bad conduct" (line 459). This links disability and poverty to moral fault.
  - Opposite-sex exclusion (line 86), which assumes heterosexual attraction.
  - Jātaka ideals of feeling no anger while mutilated (lines 198–228). These must not become a norm for victims of violence.
  - Supernormal protection from fire, poison and weapons (line 443), and deities (lines 429–441).
  - Margaya lines 33 and 40: gods and Brahmās as objects of compassion and gladness.
