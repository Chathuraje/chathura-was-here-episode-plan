# C007 — The two hatred-rooted mind-states

## Sources and coverage

- `Abhidhammattha Pradeepika.md`: read in full (lines 1–65). Frontmatter declares Book 1, chapter 1, PDF 49–51 (printed 38–40). The extract starts mid-page on PDF 49 / printed 38 (line 21). Page markers at line 36 (PDF 50 / printed 39) and line 55 (PDF 51 / printed 40).
- `Abhidharma Margaya.md`: read in full (lines 1–23). Heading `# 8. ද්වේෂ මූල සිත් දෙක` (the Margaya's own numbering is one ahead of the concept number). No page locators.

## Central meaning

Hatred-rooted consciousness (ද්වේෂ / පටිඝ මූල සිත) has two types. Both always come with painful mental feeling (දෝමනස්ස). They differ only in whether they arise unprompted (අසංඛාරික) or prompted (සසංඛාරික). Hatred is the mind's opposition to its object: not wanting it, as if striking against it (Margaya line 3). Pradeepika (lines 32–34) describes the sequence: meeting a disagreeable object, displeasure and a "soiled" or painful quality arising in the mind, and a wish to remove, destroy or harm the object because it is felt as hostile.

## Essential distinctions

- **Scope of the word.** Margaya (line 3) groups anger, rage, enmity, fear, aversion and discontent under hatred, and says sorrow (ශෝක) is the painful feeling itself. Pradeepika (line 51) places envy (ඊර්ෂ්‍යා: not tolerating others' prosperity), stinginess (මච්ඡරිය: hiding one's goods so they do not go to others) and regret (කුක්කුච්ච: later remorse over wrong done and good not done) in these same states.
- **Conditions** (Pradeepika lines 40–45): meeting a disagreeable object, an irritable temperament, lack of learning, and not reflecting far on the object.
- **Effects** (Pradeepika line 47): right and wrong, good and bad cease to be visible; burning in the mind; bodily dryness and change of colour; weeping; harsh words; striking one's own head and chest; striking a nearby person or thing; killing others or harming oneself.
- **Greed turning into hatred** (Pradeepika line 59): a person craving an animal's meat cannot fulfil the wish while the animal lives. The wish to kill the obstacle arises, and "from here onwards" the states are hatred-rooted.
- **Subtle hatred** (Margaya line 11): killing animals for sport while laughing is still hatred-rooted; the hatred is subtle, whereas killing an attacking enemy involves fierce hatred.
- **Occasions** (Margaya lines 17–23): speaking or listening to others' faults; hearing one's own faults or weaknesses named; disobedient servants; seeing or anticipating others' wrongdoing; others handling what one cherishes; being asked for what one loves; being refused; others not doing or accepting what one says; enmity and remembering past enmity; grief at the death of parents or children, other losses and one's own great mistakes; envy on seeing those who eat, dress, travel, live or are honoured better; habitual fault-finding.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

Both sources agree on two types, painful feeling, and the prompted/unprompted division (Pradeepika 25–28, 38, 63; Margaya 5–9). Pradeepika says killing, harsh speech and ill will are fixed (නියත) in this state while theft, lying, slander and idle talk are not fixed (lines 53, 57). Margaya (line 15) simply lists all seven courses of action as occasions. This is complementary rather than conflicting, since "not fixed" means they can occur. Margaya adds fear and the sport-killing case; Pradeepika adds conditions, bodily effects and the greed-to-hatred example. Both include grief (Pradeepika 49; Margaya 19). Neither source contradicts the other.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

The claims that these states, frequent near death, lead to the four lower realms (Pradeepika line 51), and the moral classification of grief and regret as unwholesome, are doctrinal. The classification must never be used to label grieving or regretful people as at fault. The bodily effects are a traditional description, not a medical account. The class-bound example of "disobedient servants" should not be carried as a norm.

## Human meaning paragraph

The sources describe a family of reactions that share one movement: the mind meets something it does not want and pushes against it. The same push can look like rage, fear, envy, sorrow or regret. It can grow out of desire when something stands in the way, and it can hide inside laughter. The source claims that while this push dominates, a person loses sight of right and wrong. The everyday reading below is interpretation: resistance to what is unwanted is a shared thread linking reactions that people usually treat as unrelated.

## Idea decisions

- **C007-I01 — When an obstacle to wanting becomes an enemy.** Accepted. Based on Pradeepika's meat example (line 59) and the "hostile object" description (line 34).
- **C007-I02 — Unable to bear another person's good fortune.** Accepted. Based on Pradeepika's definition of envy (line 51) and Margaya's comparison occasions (line 21).
- **C007-I03 — Grief as pushing against what happened.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy, with strong ethical limits. Based on Margaya line 19, Pradeepika line 49 and the definitions of sorrow and opposition (Margaya line 3).
- Rejected candidate: *Harm done while laughing* (Margaya line 11). Only one sentence, unexplained, and tied to killing animals; it sits awkwardly with the claim that the feeling is always painful. Kept as a held question.
- Rejected candidate: *Hearing one's own faults named* (Margaya line 17). A genuine occasion but only listed, with no mechanism given; noted as a possible future card if another concept develops it.
- Rejected candidate: regret as a form of aversion (Pradeepika line 51). Listed only; too thin and ethically sensitive.
