## Central meaning

Consciousness is one in nature but is divided, for teaching purposes, into 89 (brief) or 121 (detailed) types (Margaya line 5; Pradeepika line 75). Pradeepika first gives background on the mind: a being has not one mind but a stream of mind-moments (සිත් පරම්පරාව) that arise and cease so fast, and without gaps, that they seem to be one mind (line 25). One mind-moment takes one object, and one aspect of that object, at a time (line 25). What feels like seeing, hearing, moving and speaking all at once is a vast number of separate moments in rapid succession (lines 49–53). It then lists eight ways of dividing consciousness: by plane (භූමි), by kind (කුසල්, අකුසල්, විපාක, ක්‍රියා), by feeling, by association, by prompting, mundane/supramundane, with or without roots, and beautiful/non-beautiful (lines 77–87), and defines each (lines 89–153).

## Essential distinctions

- **Stream and moment.** Continuity is an appearance produced by speed and gaplessness (Pradeepika lines 25, 49–53). Each moment has arising, standing and dissolving phases (line 55).
- **Conditions for a mind-moment:** a preceding stream (line 31, mango-tree analogy) and an object (line 33, walking-stick analogy: as a weak person needs a stick to rise, a mind needs an object).
- **Rebirth-linking, life-continuum and death consciousness** (lines 37–45) and the near-death process (line 41). Doctrinal.
- **Seat of the mind** (lines 57–69). Pradeepika argues against the brain as the seat of mind, placing it in the heart-base, and cites foetal development in weeks and babies born without a brain. It concludes that the brain sustains the body and sense faculties, and that an afflicted, exhausted body weakens the mind-stream (line 69).
- **Power of mind** (line 71): knowledge of past lives, others' minds, flying and other powers. Doctrinal.
- **Prompting.** Unprompted states arise keenly by nature without urging; prompted states arise through one's own or another's urging (lines 135–137). Pradeepika adds a condition: unprompted states arise when mind and body are at ease and there is skill and good practice in the work; prompted states arise when mind and body are exhausted and heavy (line 139).
- **Feeling** (lines 113–125): every consciousness "tastes" its object; bodily pleasure and pain, mental joy and displeasure, and neutral feeling.
- **Roots** (lines 145–149): greed, hatred and delusion, and their opposites, steady the mind on its object as roots steady a tree.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

Both sources agree on the fourfold division by plane and the totals 54, 15, 12 and 8/40 (Margaya line 29). Both say the plane names mean "mostly arising in" that plane, and that such minds can occur elsewhere (Margaya line 19; Pradeepika line 97). Margaya adds that "supramundane plane" is not a place (line 17). Pradeepika gives far more background (stream, lifespan, seat, power, the eight divisions). Apparent numerical difference: Margaya speaks of fifteen fine-material planes as places where such minds mostly arise (line 13), while Pradeepika counts sixteen and notes that one of them has no mind (line 95). These are consistent once the mindless plane is excluded; not a real conflict.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

Rebirth-linking, death consciousness, near-death objects, 31 planes, infinite world-systems, supernormal powers and the heart-base are doctrinal. Pradeepika's discussion of the brain, foetal weeks and anencephalic infants (lines 63–65) is a historical, doctrinally motivated physiological argument and must not be presented as science or used to support any idea. The claim that billions of mind-moments occur in a finger-snap (line 53) is doctrinal and must not be equated with neuroscience of attention or perception timing. The karmic ranking of prompted and unprompted states is not a measure of effort or guilt.

## Human meaning paragraph

Two human-level observations survive simplification. First, what feels like doing many things at once may be experience switching rapidly between single objects; the feeling of one continuous mind is itself something produced. This is interpretation beyond the source's doctrinal claim, and it is only a limited analogy. Second, whether an action arises readily or needs urging is linked to the state of mind and body and to practised skill: ease and skill go with readiness, exhaustion and heaviness with needing to be pushed. The source applies this to all prompted and unprompted states, wholesome and unwholesome, not only to desire.

## Idea decisions

- **C003-I01 — Many things at once, one thing at a time.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on Pradeepika lines 25 and 49–53, with Margaya's drum analogy in C002 as supporting material.
- **C003-I02 — When a tired body makes every act need pushing.** Accepted. Based on Pradeepika line 139, with lines 135–137 and line 69. Related to C006-I03 but a different mechanism: C006-I03 is about who does the pushing in a greed-rooted want; this card is about the bodily and practical conditions that make any action need pushing.
- Rejected candidate: *Searching for a first cause that cannot be found* (line 31, mango-tree lineage). The passage serves a doctrinal argument against a creator and for beginningless rebirth; a secular version would substantially change it.
- Rejected candidate: *The mind needs something to lean on* (line 33, walking stick). Too general; restates C002.
- Rejected candidate: *A worn-out body wears down the mind* (line 69). Embedded in the heart-base versus brain argument; its human content is absorbed into C003-I02 only as secondary support.
- Rejected candidate: anything from the power of mind (line 71), planes (lines 89–97) or kamma-result analogy (lines 105–109). Doctrinal.
