# C093 — Mindfulness of the body, or contemplation of repulsiveness

## Central meaning

Mindfulness of the body (කායගතාසති) is here the meditation on thirty-two body parts (ද්වත්තිංසාකාර): hair, body hair, nails, teeth, skin, flesh and so on to urine. The meditator learns the list from a teacher through seven kinds of learning skill (උග්ගහ කොසල්ල): reciting aloud, reciting mentally, and noting each part's colour, shape, direction (above or below the navel), location and boundary. Ten kinds of attention skill (මනසිකාර කොසල්ල) follow: keep the order, go neither too fast nor too slow, keep the mind from straying, go beyond the names, drop the parts that do not become clear, reach absorption, and the three suttas on balancing the mind. Each part is then seen as repulsive in five ways: colour, shape, smell, origin (ආශ්‍රය / නිශ්‍රය) and location. The learning image is the parts as noted; the counterpart image is their repulsiveness. The practice leads to the first absorption only, because the object is coarse, and can be turned into insight.

## Essential distinctions

- **Repulsiveness versus elements.** Repulsiveness (පටික්කූල) is calm-meditation (සමථ); seeing the same parts as elements is insight (විපස්සනා) (Book 4 line 113; Pradeepika lines 114–118).
- **Words first, then beyond words.** Recitation aloud supports mental recitation, which supports understanding of the repulsive or element characteristic (Book 4 line 96; Pradeepika line 100). Later the names "hair", "body hair" are passed over and the mind rests on the repulsive aspect itself. The simile is people who mark a forest well with palm leaves and drop the marker once their feet have worn a path (Book 4 lines 145–149; Pradeepika line 145).
- **Pace and order.** Skipping parts is like an unskilled man climbing a 32-rung ladder by missing rungs and tiring out. Too fast is like a traveller who reaches the end without knowing the road. Too slow is like one who dawdles at ponds and never arrives (Book 4 lines 133–141; Pradeepika lines 135–141).
- **Dropping what does not appear.** Parts that do not become clear are let go, one at a time, until one part is held. The simile is a monkey fleeing a hunter through 32 palms that finally clings to one (Pradeepika lines 151–157; Book 4 line 151).
- **Balancing.** The goldsmith simile (blowing, sprinkling, watching) and the six conditions of the Sītibhāva sutta (restrain, exert, gladden, look on) (Pradeepika lines 161–193). Book 4 (line 159) refers back to this passage without repeating it.
- **Joy despite a repulsive object.** The first absorption is joyful because the meditator sees the benefit (Pradeepika line 209).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

All three sources agree on the 32 parts, recitation forward and backward, the attainment of the first absorption and the route to insight. Margaya gives a teaching method: six groups, several days each, until the parts appear in the mind as the words are spoken (lines 9–23). Pradeepika adds the wider body-contemplation frame: the fourteen sections, the car and city similes of whole and parts (lines 30–36), and the removal of covetousness and distress. Book 4 adds a very detailed description of each part (lines 171–320) and the benefits: overcoming discontent and delight, fear and dread, and cold and heat (line 334).

Minor differences:

- **Order of the list.** Book 4 line 51 lists pihaka before kilomaka and puts the brain last. Its recitation order (lines 80–92) and Margaya line 7 put the brain in the fourth group. This is complementary, not a conflict.
- **The Kāyagatāsati sutta.** Pradeepika line 116 says it teaches the parts as elements. Book 4 line 113 adds that it teaches four absorptions on the parts as colour kasiṇa for those to whom they appear as colour. Book 4 line 334 agrees ("four absorptions by taking colour differences"), while Pradeepika line 211 says repulsiveness gives only the first absorption. These fit together, because different routes lead to different results.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

- The claim that the body is inherently foul and contains nothing clean is a doctrinal contemplation aimed at countering lust (Pradeepika line 199). It must not become a claim about bodies, hygiene or body image.
- Seeing all people and animals as "heaps of corpse-parts" rather than beings (Book 4 line 326; Pradeepika line 205) is a meditative perception. As an everyday attitude it would dehumanise people.
- Pre-modern physiology must not be carried over. Examples: the heart-blood's colour by temperament and liver size by wisdom (Book 4 lines 231–237), 32 kinds of stomach worms (line 261), intestines of 32 cubits in men and 28 in women (line 249), bile disorder causing madness and loss of shame (line 275), and death if the spleen is exposed (line 245).
- Other doctrinal content: past-life kasiṇa practice explaining how parts appear (Pradeepika lines 213–217), stream-entry from recitation alone (Book 4 line 71), the six higher knowledges (Book 4 line 334), and the claim that the practice is unknown outside a Buddha's dispensation (Book 4 line 26).

## Human meaning paragraph

The sources describe a slow learning method. Words are said aloud, then silently, until what they name appears clearly. Then the words are left behind, like a trail marker nobody needs once the path is worn. Pace and order matter, and parts that do not come clear are dropped in favour of the one that does. Separately, to show that the repulsiveness is not invented, Book 4 appeals to ordinary disgust. People push away food with something hair-coloured in it, throw out a fibre felt in the dark as if it were hair, and refuse vegetables grown on sewage. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that these examples show disgust responding to what a thing is believed to be and where it came from, not only to how it looks.

## Idea decisions

- **C093-I01 — Outgrowing the words that first guided learning.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on recitation aloud then mentally, going beyond the names, and the well-marker simile (all three sources).
- **C093-I02 — When the same thing disgusts once out of place.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on Book 4's everyday disgust examples for hair (lines 177–181). The source uses them as evidence of intrinsic repulsiveness. The card uses them only for what they show about disgust, and states that divergence.
- Rejected candidate: *Knowing when to push, cool or leave alone* (goldsmith simile, Sītibhāva sutta, Pradeepika lines 161–193). This is the same passage and mechanism as C076-I02 (*Knowing when to push and when to leave alone*) from the parallel C076 batch. Record C093 as supporting material for that card; no duplicate.
- Rejected candidate: *Seeing a whole as its parts* (car, city, banana trunk and "no man or woman apart from the parts", Pradeepika lines 24–36). This duplicates C001-I01, and here it also carries a dehumanising risk (see above).
- Rejected candidate: *Skipping steps or rushing makes learning fail* (ladder and traveller similes). This is close to C008-I01 (*When hurry leaves nothing fully taken in*). It is kept as supporting detail inside C093-I01, not as a separate card.
- Rejected candidate: *Letting go of what will not come clear, holding the one that does* (monkey simile). It is a meditation technique with thin human tension. Noted in C093-I01's source trace only as context.
- Rejected candidate: *Overcoming fear and discontent through body contemplation* (Book 4 line 334). It is a benefit claim without an explained mechanism.
