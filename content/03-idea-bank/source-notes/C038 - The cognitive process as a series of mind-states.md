# C038 — The cognitive process as a series of mind-states

## Central meaning

A cognitive process (චිත්ත වීථිය) is a series of mind-states arising in order, without a gap, taking one object through one of the six doors. The word "street/path" is used because the order of mind-states is like a road (Pradeepika line 29). To understand processes one must know the "six sixes": bases, doors, objects, consciousnesses, processes and modes of presentation (lines 33–41). Processes are of two kinds: five-door and mind-door (lines 47–51).

## Essential distinctions

- **No act is done by a single mind-state.** Seeing rightly, hearing rightly, speaking a word, understanding another's words, raising or lowering a limb, going, coming, giving, taking: each needs an ordered series of mind-states (Margaya line 3).
- **The mind is hard to know because the instrument of knowing is the mind itself.** A knife can cut other things but not itself; so the mind cannot know its own nature directly. Ordinary people do not even notice that cognitive processes exist; even those who study the mind know it by inference (අනුමාන), not by direct perception like seeing (Margaya line 5). The author still urges the effort, because knowledge of mind is very useful.
- The mind is invisible, not felt by the body like wind or cold, and arises and breaks too fast to be examined (Margaya line 5).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

Both define the process as an ordered series of mind-states for one object. Pradeepika is structural (six sixes, two kinds of process). Margaya is explanatory and reflective (every act is a series; limits of self-knowledge). They complement each other; no disagreement.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

The ordered sequence of mind-moments is a doctrinal model, not neuroscience. "Too fast to examine" is not a measured claim. The knife image is an argument about limits of knowledge, not a denial that introspection has any value; the author explicitly recommends inferential study.

## Human meaning paragraph

The source states two things a person could recognise in daily life. First, even a simple act such as giving something or understanding a sentence is not one event but a chain. Second, the mind trying to understand itself is like a knife trying to cut itself: people can only know their own minds indirectly, by inference, yet the effort is still worthwhile. The everyday reading (interpretation) concerns self-understanding that is always partial and inferred.
