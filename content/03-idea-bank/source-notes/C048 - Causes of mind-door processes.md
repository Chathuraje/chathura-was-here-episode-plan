# C048 — Causes of mind-door processes

## Sources and coverage

- `Abhidhammattha Pradeepika.md`: read in full (lines 1–33). Frontmatter: Book 1, chapter 4, PDF 341 (printed 331). The extract begins mid-page (line 21). Heading `### නිද්‍රාව හා ස්වප්න`. No further page marker inside the extract.
- `Abhidharma Margaya.md`: read in full (lines 1–31). Heading `# 11. මනෝද්වාර වීථි ඇති වීමේ හේතු`, subheadings `## අනුබන්ධක මනෝද්වාර වීථි.` (line 21) and `## විශේෂ කරුණක්` (line 29). No page locators.

## Central meaning

The two sources cover different material under this concept.

- **Margaya** explains why mind-door processes arise, since their causes are less obvious than those of sense processes (line 3). Causes include sense contact, association with what was sensed, hearing others speak, association with what was heard, being asked questions, reflecting (කල්පනා කිරීම, defined as directing one's mind toward matters not yet known), bodily element disturbance, hunger and thirst, bodily pains, and meeting enemies or friends (line 5). A single sight is like a bell struck once: the sound continues in a fading series, and the object "standing at" the mind door may give rise to "hundreds, thousands" of mind-door processes (line 7). Similar and connected objects also come up (line 9). Hearing that something happened somewhere, and reading books, cause countless processes, and so do the matters linked to them (lines 11–13). Questions bring many connected matters (line 15). Margaya adds that spirits (භූතයන්) sometimes give objects to people's minds (line 19).
- **Margaya, "following" processes (අනුබන්ධක, lines 23–27).** An eye process alone does not produce the knowledge "I saw such a thing"; the eye process knows only colour. Then come mind-door processes taking the past form, then its name, then the meaning of the name. The knowledge "I saw X" arises only after the third. Margaya labels this "one teacher's view" and says books describe it in several ways.
- **Pradeepika** covers sleep and dreams: one falls asleep and wakes through a mind-door process, never a sense process (line 25). Dreams occur in "monkey sleep" (කපිමිද්ධ) for all except arahants, and dream impulsions can be wholesome or unwholesome but are very weak and give only minor results (lines 27–33).

## Essential distinctions

- Sense contact versus the long tail of mind processes that follow it.
- Knowing a colour versus knowing "I saw X", which needs name and meaning.
- Reflection as directing the mind toward the not-yet-known.
- Dream acts as weak in result, not as equivalent to waking acts.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The sources are complementary rather than overlapping; only Margaya discusses causes and only Pradeepika discusses sleep. The following-process sequence in Margaya (sense, past form, name, meaning: three mind-door processes) differs from Pradeepika's sequence in C046 (sense, past form, colour, concept, then reaction: four mind-door processes). Margaya itself flags that teachers differ.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

Spirits giving objects to minds (Margaya line 19) is a doctrinal and folk claim and must not be used. "Element disturbance" (ධාතු කෝපය) is a traditional physiological account, not modern medicine. The dream claims (arahants do not dream; dream acts yield only minor results) are doctrinal. "Hundreds and thousands" of processes is illustrative, not a count.

## Human meaning paragraph

Margaya describes something familiar: one sight, a piece of news, a book or a question keeps producing thoughts long after the contact is over, like the fading hum of a bell struck once, and each thought pulls in others that resemble or connect to it. Hunger, pain and meeting a friend or an enemy also set the mind going. The everyday reading is interpretation: much of what occupies a mind is not new input but the continuing resonance and association of a few contacts.
