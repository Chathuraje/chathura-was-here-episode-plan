# C060 — The four ways death comes about

## Central meaning

Death (මරණය / චුතිය) is the end of one existence: conventionally, a being's disappearance from its group; ultimately, the breaking up of the five aggregates and the cutting of the life faculty (Pradeepika line 27). It comes about in four ways: exhaustion of the life span proper to that kind of being (ආයුක්ෂය), exhaustion of the force of the rebirth-producing action (කර්මක්ෂය), exhaustion of both together (උභයක්ෂය), and interruption while both remain (උපච්ඡේදක), called untimely death (අකාල මරණය). The first three are "timely" deaths (කාල මරණ). Pradeepika closes with a lamp simile (lines 76–82): a lamp goes out when the oil runs out, when the wick runs out, when both run out together, or when a strong wind blows it out while oil and wick remain.

## Essential distinctions

- **Running out versus being cut off.** Three deaths are exhaustion; the fourth is interruption of a life that could have continued.
- **Karma and other conditions in untimely death.** Pradeepika (lines 60–72) explains untimely death as a destructive (උපඝාතක) action from a past life cutting the rebirth action's force, given an opening by a present-life action or by conditions such as an enemy's knife, gunshot or poison weakening the body, and by careless conduct, unsuitable food, exposure to contagious disease and failure to get proper treatment (line 72). Pradeepika also says a strong rebirth action and skilled helpers can prevent a death that a present killing would otherwise bring (line 62–66).
- **Margaya's broader account** (lines 21–35). Untimely death happens "not only through karma but through many other causes". Life and karma are both weak; without food, drink and protection, karma alone cannot sustain life; life stands amid dangers inside and outside the body (line 23). It lists Nāgasena's eight causes: wind, bile, phlegm, their combination, change of climate (floods, drought and famine, line 29), careless handling of the body (being hit by vehicles, caught in fire, falling into water, line 31), assault by oneself or others (line 33), and result of action. It states: "Taking everything as happening only through karma is a wrong view", and "therefore if someone dies untimely, one cannot know whether it happened through karma or through some other cause" (line 35).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

Both sources agree on the fourfold list and on the definitions of the three timely deaths. Margaya adds that lifespan depends on the parents' bodies, food and the quality of the season, and lengthens or shortens as wholesome or unwholesome roots grow among people (line 7); it also reports that beings with karmic force can extend life by psychic power or alchemical medicine (line 9). Margaya says simultaneous exhaustion of both is rare (line 17). **Difference in emphasis:** Pradeepika frames outside conditions mainly as openings through which destructive karma delivers its result; Margaya treats them as causes in their own right, explicitly rejects a karma-only explanation, and says the cause of a particular untimely death cannot be known. Pradeepika's list of where untimely death does and does not occur (line 74) has no counterpart in Margaya.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

Karmic force, rebirth-producing actions, past-life killings, fixed lifespans of classes of beings, the realms where untimely death cannot occur, and life extension by psychic power or alchemy are doctrinal claims. The humoral causes (wind, bile, phlegm) are a historical medical framework, not modern medicine. Margaya line 33 mentions self-inflicted death with specific methods; this is not used as an idea premise and must be handled with great care in any later research.

## Human meaning paragraph

When a life ends, people want to know why. The sources distinguish a life that runs its course from a life that is cut short, and one of them insists that an early death usually has many interacting causes (illness, climate, accident, violence, neglect and, doctrinally, past action) and that the particular cause cannot be known from outside. The everyday reading below is interpretation: survivors often reach for one decisive explanation, such as fate or someone's fault, and the text resists both. A second, related reading is that believing an outcome is fixed can make ordinary precautions seem pointless, while the text treats care, food, protection and treatment as genuinely affecting when a life ends.

## Idea decisions

- **C060-I01 — Asking why a life ended too soon.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on Margaya lines 23 and 35 and the lamp simile.
- **C060-I02 — Leaving to fate what care could change.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on Margaya lines 23, 31 and 35 and Pradeepika line 72. Distinct from I01: I01 concerns explanation after a death; I02 concerns action and neglect before one.
- Rejected candidate: *The lamp that goes out four ways* as a card of its own. It is an image that serves I01, not a separate mechanism.
- Rejected candidate: self-inflicted death (Margaya line 33). Too sensitive to derive from a single list item, and the source gives no mechanism beyond classification.
- Rejected candidate: lifespan shortening as morality declines (Margaya line 7). Doctrinal, no human process described.
