# C047 — Occasions when mind-door processes arise

## Sources and coverage

- `Abhidhammattha Pradeepika.md`: read in full (lines 1–37). Frontmatter: Book 1, chapter 4, PDF 333–334 (printed 323–324). The extract begins mid-page on PDF 333 / printed 323 (line 21); page marker at line 29 (PDF 334 / printed 324).
- `Abhidharma Margaya.md`: read in full (lines 1–25). Heading `# 10. මනෝද්වාර වීථි ඇති වන අවස්ථා`. No page locators.

## Central meaning

Anything can become an object of the mind door: present, past or future mind, mental factors and matter, and timeless nibbāna and concepts (Pradeepika line 25). The mind door itself is the arrest-bhavaṅga moment that meets the object. Something seen, heard or otherwise contacted (දිට්ඨ, සුත, මුත) can be taken again by the mind (line 31). Objects are clear or faint, and mind-door processes are of sense-sphere or absorption impulsion (lines 35–37).

## Essential distinctions

Margaya works through a single example, a visit to the Ruvanvæli stupa:

- **Seeing** the stupa is an eye-door process (line 5).
- **Recalling the stupa at home** is a mind-door process whose object is the past matter that was seen. That matter has already ceased; "now it is not" (lines 7–9).
- **Bowing toward the stupa from home** takes the stupa as it exists at that moment, not the one seen earlier, because everything seen earlier has ceased (line 11).
- **Recalling the name** "Ruvanvæli stupa" takes a concept as object (line 15).
- **Recalling that you saw the stupa** takes the earlier eye-consciousness as object, a mind taking a mind. "Recalling the stupa is one thing; recalling that you saw the stupa is another. Understand the difference without confusing the two" (line 17).
- **Recalling the joy** felt on seeing it takes the mental factor of joy as object (line 19).
- **Recalling a pain**, and **feeling another person's pain when you see them suffering**, both take the mental factor of feeling as object (line 21). Likewise love, anger, conceit, jealousy, loving-kindness and compassion, whether recalled in oneself or sensed in others (line 23).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The two sources agree on the range of mind-door objects and on seen, heard and contacted objects being retaken by the mind. Pradeepika is the concise technical statement; Margaya supplies all the lived examples. No disagreement found.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

The seventeen-moment lifespan of matter and the claim that the seen stupa has "ceased" are doctrinal momentariness, not physics. Margaya's statement that the moment-by-moment age of the present stupa's matter determines which of the seven processes arises (line 13) is technical and not carried forward. Worship at a distance is described as a mental act, not a claim about the stupa's power.

## Human meaning paragraph

Margaya separates several things we usually blur. Remembering a thing, remembering its name, remembering that we saw it, and remembering how we felt when we saw it each take a different object. What we remember is an appearance that no longer exists, and the thing itself has moved on. The text also treats sensing another person's pain or love as the mind taking their feeling as an object, which is different from seeing their body. The everyday reading is interpretation: memory of a place, memory of one's own experience there, and inference about another's inner state are distinct acts, and they can come apart.

- **C047-I02 — Sensing another's pain you cannot see.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on Margaya lines 21–23. The source states the classification but not how accurate such sensing is; that tension is my interpretation and is labelled.
