# C095 — The four divine abidings

## Sources and coverage

- `Abhidhammattha Pradeepika Book 4.md`: read in full (lines 1–616). Frontmatter: Book 4, chapter 9 (කම්මට්ඨාන සංග්‍රහ විභාගය), PDF 245–292 (printed 226–273). Page markers run from line 22 (PDF 245 / printed 226, mid-page start) to line 607 (PDF 292 / printed 273). Lines 527–616 cover the perfections and the Buddha's ten powers, four confidences, six unique knowledges and eighteen qualities. The inclusion note (line 13) explains that they are kept here as the fruit of the divine abidings.
- `Abhidharma Margaya.md`: read in full (lines 1–47). Heading `# 11. බ්‍ර‍හ්මවිහාර භාවනා`, lesson 11, with subheadings for each abiding. No page locators.

## Central meaning

The four divine abidings (බ්‍රහ්මවිහාර) are goodwill (මෙත්තා), compassion (කරුණා), gladness at others' happiness (මුදිතා) and equanimity (උපෙක්ඛා). They are called "immeasurables" (අප්පමඤ්ඤා) because they are extended to all beings without limit. Book 4 calls them practices for others' welfare, unlike other meditation subjects that serve only one's own welfare (line 33). Each has a characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause. Each also has a success (සම්පත්ති) and a failure (විපත්ති): craving-based affection for goodwill, sorrow for compassion, worldly laughter for gladness, and ignorant indifference for equanimity (lines 45–60). Each counters a dominant tendency: ill-will, cruelty, discontent (අරති) and lust (line 39). A mother with four sons illustrates the four attitudes (line 39). She wants the infant to grow, wants the sick son to be cured, wants the young son's prime to last, and does not worry about the son busy with his own affairs.

## Essential distinctions

- **Goodwill versus affection rooted in craving** (Book 4 line 45). Goodwill is care joined with understanding. Craving presents itself as affection and is its failure.
- **Order of persons.** Do not begin with a disliked person (tiring), a very dear friend (a slight pain of theirs makes one lament), a neutral person (hard to hold as dear) or an enemy (anger arises). Do not direct it individually at someone of the opposite sex (lust can arise; the minister's son who "fought the wall" all night) or at the dead (Book 4 lines 73–90; Margaya line 14). Begin with oneself only as a witness ("as I want happiness, so do others"). Goodwill to oneself alone never yields absorption (lines 92–98; Margaya line 16). Then go from a respected teacher-like figure, to a very dear friend, to a neutral person, to an enemy. Make the mind soft and workable at each step before moving on (lines 100–110).
- **Methods against resentment toward an enemy** (lines 116–261). Return to an easier person and re-enter goodwill. Recall the Buddha's sayings, such as the saw simile, the seven things an enemy wishes on an angry person and the funeral-pyre log simile. Recall only the calm part of the enemy's conduct and ignore the rest. If there is none, feel compassion (doctrinally, for their future in hell). Admonish oneself. Reflect on ownership of action (කම්මස්සකතා). Recall the Bodhisatta's forbearance in Jātaka stories. Reflect that the enemy has been one's mother or father in past lives. Recall the benefits of goodwill. Analyse the enemy into elements and aggregates. Finally, exchange gifts.
- **Breaking the boundaries** (සීමාසම්භේද, lines 263–271). Bandits demand one of four people: oneself, a dear person, a neutral person or an enemy. If one would hand over anyone, oneself included, the boundaries are not broken. Only when none would be handed over is the mind equal toward all.
- **Compassion** starts with someone in obvious misery, not with a dear person, because the dearness does not fall away (line 459). **Gladness** starts with a cheerful close companion, because merely dear people do not trigger it (lines 481–487). **Equanimity** requires prior absorption through the other three and starts with a naturally neutral person (lines 493–497). Its basis is seeing that beings own their actions, so no one's wish makes them happy or frees them (lines 60, 497; Margaya line 47).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The sources agree on the definitions, the order of persons, oneself as witness, the failure to reach absorption on oneself, and equanimity's basis in ownership of action. Margaya adds the dangers of hatred and the benefits of patience in vivid lists (lines 10–12). It also says insincere wishing ("May they be happy" without really wanting it) has no power (line 18). Book 4 gives the full commentary: the enemy methods, the Jātaka exempla, the eleven benefits, the ranges of pervasion and the relation to the formless attainments.

Differences in emphasis:

- **Starting person.** For goodwill, Margaya (line 14) says to choose a teacher-like or good person and to avoid mother, father and siblings as "too dear". Book 4 (line 100) similarly begins with a respected teacher-like figure. They are consistent.
- **Compassion toward the apparently happy.** Margaya grounds this in their suffering after death and in gods falling from heaven (line 33). Book 4 uses the condemned thief given treats on the way to execution (lines 461–465). Both are doctrinal.
- **Equanimity's absorption.** Margaya gives the fifth absorption (line 45). Book 4 gives the fourth or fifth depending on the counting system (line 509). These are consistent.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

- **Ownership of action as the basis of equanimity** (Margaya line 47; Book 4 lines 60, 188–192, 497). In everyday use it could suggest that people deserve their suffering, or excuse not helping. This must be flagged whenever equanimity is discussed.
- Rebirth and cosmology: compassion for an enemy because of future hell (line 160), all beings having been one's parents (lines 230–236), gods and Brahmā worlds (Margaya lines 33 and 40), and rebirth in the Brahmā world as a benefit (line 455).
- Supernormal benefits: fire, poison and weapons do not harm the practitioner, a cow's love for her calf making a spear shrivel (line 443), and deities guiding and weeping (lines 429–439).
- The Jātaka exempla (lines 198–228) are legendary. Their ideal of not even feeling anger while being mutilated must not become a norm for victims of violence.
- The opposite-sex rule assumes heterosexual attraction. The compassion example of a mutilated beggar "with bad conduct" (line 459) links disability and poverty to moral fault.
- The Buddha's powers (lines 533–616) have no human-idea use.

## Human meaning paragraph

The sources treat care for others as a skill that can go wrong in recognisable ways. Affection can turn into wanting, pity into sorrow, shared joy into mere hilarity, and even-mindedness into not caring. Goodwill is built step by step. It starts from the person easiest to wish well and moves toward the one who is hardest. When anger rises, the practitioner retreats to an easier person and returns later. A long set of reflections works on resentment. One of them notes that staying angry brings on the angry person the very things an enemy would wish for them. Equanimity is described through a mother who stops fussing over a grown son busy with his own life. It rests on recognising that one's wishing cannot live another's life for them. My everyday reading of these points is interpretation, and the ownership-of-action basis is doctrinal.

## Idea decisions

- **C095-I01 — Growing goodwill from the easiest person outward.** Accepted for research. Based on the order of persons, oneself as witness, retreating to an easier person when anger rises, and breaking the boundaries.
- **C095-I02 — When staying angry gives the rival what they want.** Accepted for research. Based on the seven things an enemy wishes on an angry person and the related self-admonitions (lines 126–186).
- **C095-I03 — Stepping back as care, not as indifference.** Accepted for research as a limited application. Based on the mother simile, the definition and failure of equanimity, and the reflection that wishing does not change another's life. Its doctrinal basis in ownership of action is flagged in Limits.
- Rejected candidate: *Sorrow mistaken for compassion* (failure of compassion). This duplicates C024-I03. C095 is supporting material (Book 4 line 51).
- Rejected candidate: *Affection that slides into wanting* (failure of goodwill, line 45; the minister's son, line 86). This is close to C005-I02 (*Love that is also holding on*). C095 is supporting material.
- Rejected candidate: *Remembering the one calm trait of someone you resent* (lines 150–156). It is a genuine technique of selective attention, but thinner than I02 and partly doctrinal where it turns to hell. It is held as a possible later merge into C095-I02.
- Rejected candidate: *Giving a gift to end a quarrel* (line 261). It is vivid, but rests on one exemplum and the claim that anger "certainly" subsides. It is recorded as a held question.
- Rejected candidate: *Whom are you angry at, the hair or the elements?* (line 257). It is analysis into parts, and it duplicates the C001-I01 mechanism applied to resentment.
- Rejected candidate: *Gladness is easier with a cheerful friend* (line 483). It overlaps C030-I02 and C007-I02.
- Rejected candidate: *Wishing without meaning it has no power* (Margaya line 18). It is interesting, but a single sentence without mechanism. It is noted in I01's trace.
