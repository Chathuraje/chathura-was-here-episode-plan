# C092 — Meditation on the recollection of death

Sinhala title: මරණානුස්මෘති භාවනාව.

## Central meaning

Recollection of death is reflecting that oneself and every other being will certainly die, that no power frees anyone from it, and that the time is uncertain. Book 4 classifies death: death as the cutting off of the life faculty, the arahant's final cutting off, momentary death and conventional death ("the tree died"). It divides the first into timely and untimely death. It then says the practice must be done with mindfulness, a sense of urgency (සංවේගය) and understanding. Done carelessly, it produces grief (for loved ones), gladness (for enemies), indifference (for strangers) or fear (for oneself). If this does not settle the mind, there are eight modes of reflection: the executioner standing by, success ending in ruin, comparison with the great, the body shared with many dangers, the frailty of life, signlessness (no fixed span, disease, time, place or destination), the short span of human life, and the brevity of a moment. The practice reaches only access concentration. Its stated benefits are diligence, disenchantment, less attachment to life, less hoarding, freedom from stinginess over possessions, and dying without fear or confusion.

## Essential distinctions

- **Unwise versus wise recollection** (Book 4 line 80). The same object (a death) produces grief, gladness, indifference or fear depending on one's relation to the dead person, unless it is held with mindfulness, urgency and understanding.
- **Bringing it home by comparison** (lines 119–151). The famous, meritorious, strong, powerful, wise, and even Buddhas all died. "Why not me?" Line 113 adds that death neither spares the high for their birth, beauty, virtue or learning nor excludes the low out of contempt.
- **Sluggish versus keen recollection** (lines 187–191). Wishing to live a day, or for four or five mouthfuls, to practise counts as "sluggish". Recollecting that one may live only for one mouthful, or one breath, counts as "diligent".
- **Signlessness** (lines 163–181). Five unknowables: life span, the illness that kills, the time of death, where the body falls, and the destination.
- **Access, not absorption** (line 204). Death is a real phenomenon and it stirs urgency, so the mind does not reach full absorption.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

- Both sources centre on the certainty of death and the uncertainty of its time. Margaya's single reflection (lines 3–9) compresses several of Book 4's modes: the mighty who died (comparison), cattle led to slaughter and daily approach (life's short span; Book 4 line 185), fruit, dewdrops, bubbles and foam (lines 103, 185), and death at every age from the womb onward (signlessness; line 167). Margaya adds that "those who live long suffer immeasurable ageing" and "this death could come even today" (line 9).
- Only Book 4 gives the types of death, the warning about unwise recollection, the eight modes, the limit at access concentration and the benefits.
- No substantive disagreement was found.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

Timely and untimely death defined by kamma or merit running out (lines 50–76), the claim that people are usually conceived at night (line 103), destination after death and "a good destination" as a benefit (line 210), and momentary death as the break-up of each mind-moment (lines 193–202) are doctrinal. The meditation's claimed effects are the text's claims, not verified outcomes. Death reflection has clinical and ethical risks for bereaved, ill or suicidal people. Any research must be handled with care and must not present this practice as advice. Line 67 mentions suicide as one kind of untimely death. This must not be used as material.

## Human meaning paragraph

The texts describe how thinking about death is shaped by relationship. The death of someone loved brings grief, an enemy's death brings a kind of gladness, a stranger's death leaves people unmoved, and one's own death brings fear. The sources claim that a steadier way of holding the fact is possible. They suggest two practical routes: bringing death home by remembering that even the most powerful and admired people died, and shortening the horizon one assumes one has, from "a day" to "a breath". The sources link the second to less hoarding, less clinging to possessions and less fear. Applications to grief, ageing, care work or public mourning are my extensions and require evidence.

## Idea decisions

- **C092-I01 — Whose death we picture shapes what we feel.** Accepted for research. Based on the account of unwise recollection.
- **C092-I02 — Measuring your own death against the mighty.** Accepted for research. Based on the comparison mode and Margaya's reflection.
- **C092-I03 — Living as though only a breath remains.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on the sluggish and keen recollection passage, signlessness and the stated benefits.
- Rejected: *the body shared with worms, snakes and illness* (line 155). A vivid image that overlaps C060-I01's many-causes framing. The worm description is doctrinal and anatomical.
- Rejected: *success always ends in ruin* (lines 109–117). Too close to C032-I01 (pleasure turning into pain by ending) and C052-I03, and it risks becoming a slogan.
- Rejected: *momentary death*. Doctrinal analysis of mind-moments.
