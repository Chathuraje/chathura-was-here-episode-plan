# C032-I03 — Pain in the body versus distress in the mind

**Status:** Held. The distinction is sourced, but the human lesson it suggests is not stated in this source and carries an ethical risk. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Physical pain and mental distress are treated as two different feelings. Bodily pain belongs to one sensory moment. Mental distress arises only together with aversion.

## Human mechanism and tension

When feelings are listed as faculties, the source deliberately separates bodily pleasure from mental joy, and bodily pain from mental distress, "to show" (දක්වීම පිණිසම) the difference between the physical and the mental. Bodily pain occurs in a single type of mind-state: a resultant touch-consciousness. Mental distress occurs only in the two aversion-rooted states. An obvious reading is that pain in the body need not become distress in the mind, and that distress involves some resisting of what is felt. That reading is my extension. This source does not state it, and it does not describe any movement from one to the other.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction, pending review: people who live with physical pain and describe their relationship to it differently over time. The research would ask how they tell apart "the hurting" and "the being upset about hurting", if they do at all. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

Is the hurt of the body the same thing as being troubled by it?

## Limits and alternatives

The card is held for three reasons. First, the source classifies; it does not teach a method or claim that distress can be avoided. Second, labelling distress "aversion-rooted", which is unwholesome in the source's ethics, could imply that people in pain are at fault for suffering. That would violate the instruction not to morally label subjects. Third, a real link may need C007 (the two aversion-rooted states) or later chapters. Alternatives: distress may reflect fear about the future, loss of livelihood or lack of care, not a response to pain as such. Some modern pain research frames body–mind relations differently, and this source must not be equated with it.
