# C075-I03 — When shamelessness is as steady as conscience

**Status:** Held. The source gives definitions without an example or explanation, and the card risks morally labelling people. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

A sense of shame about doing wrong can be firm enough that nothing shakes it. The absence of that shame can be equally firm, so appeals to conscience simply fail to reach it.

## Human mechanism and tension

The sources define a "power" as a quality that suppresses its opposite and cannot be shaken by it. Shame and fear of wrongdoing are powers because shamelessness and recklessness cannot move them. Symmetrically, shamelessness and fearlessness of wrongdoing are also powers, because shame and fear cannot move them. Powers arise only where effort is present. The tension, in my interpretation, is that we treat conscience as the strong side and its absence as weakness, while the source describes two firm positions. Where shamelessness is strong, reminders of shame may not work. The source does not explain how either side becomes strong.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction, not yet approved: people who have tried to confront wrongdoing, such as whistle-blowers, community organisers or journalists, describing what happened when public shaming did not move those responsible, and what, if anything, did. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What can reach someone once being ashamed no longer does?

## Limits and alternatives

Held because the source offers only paired definitions, "power" is a mind-state term rather than a character trait, and documentary use risks labelling people shameless. Apparent indifference may instead reflect a different moral framework, a belief that the act was justified (compare C006-I02), fear, institutional protection or hidden conflict. Before acceptance, check whether the mental-factor concepts on shame and fear of wrongdoing explain the mechanism.
