# C059-I02 — Giving away only what you would not keep

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Two gifts of the same size can differ sharply. It matters whether the giver hands over what they would not want themselves, shares what they themselves use, or keeps the lesser part so that others get the better.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source names three kinds of giver using food as its example. One eats the sweet food and gives away what is not sweet; the source calls this person a "slave of giving". One gives whatever they themselves eat, sweet or not, and is a "companion in giving". One eats the plain food and treats others with the sweet, and is the "master of giving". It adds that careless, disrespectful giving through someone else is unworthy, while careful, respectful giving with one's own hands is worthy. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that the measure here is not the amount given but its relation to what the giver keeps. The tension is that giving leftovers still helps and is still giving, yet people often sense a difference in dignity for the one who receives.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: households, kitchens or communities that regularly share food or goods, such as almsgiving, feeding workers or guests, or sharing a harvest. The research would ask how people decide what to give and what to keep, and what receivers notice. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

Does what we keep for ourselves say more about a gift than what we hand over?

## Limits and alternatives

This is a doctrinal classification of donors, not a rule that people should deprive themselves. Keeping the better share may be necessary for someone with little, and giving surplus is often the only realistic option. Differences in what is given can reflect custom, health needs or what the receiver prefers rather than the giver's attitude.
