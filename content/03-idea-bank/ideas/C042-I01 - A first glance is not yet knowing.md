# C042-I01 — A first glance is not yet knowing

**Status:** Merged into C034-I01 (*Seeing before knowing what you see*), which states the same mechanism from the function chapter. This card keeps its ID and records the extra C042 evidence: Margaya's explicit claim that seeing registers only colour, so knowledge stays incomplete until impulsion "completes seeing", and the mango simile. C040-I02 also uses the simile. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

The moment something is seen is not the moment it is known. Recognising what a thing is, and deciding what to do with it, is built up in further steps after the first contact.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source says the first act of seeing registers only colour and then passes, so knowledge of the object is "not complete". Accepting, examining and settling what the thing is follow, and only after that is the object "taken well" and responded to. Each step does only its own small part. The source illustrates this with a sleeper woken by a falling mango. He looks, picks it up, squeezes it to test ripeness, smells it to be sure, then eats. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that people often treat a glance as if it were already knowledge. They act on the colour, shape or sound before the checking steps have done their work. The tension is between the speed at which we feel we "know" and the layered process the source describes.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: work where people are trained to look twice before deciding, such as sorting produce at a market, checking gems or tea leaves, reading an X-ray, or identifying a bird call. Research could ask what a first look gives them and what only the second or third step reveals. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What do we believe we have seen before we have actually finished seeing it?

## Limits and alternatives

The source describes events within a split moment, and the mango story is only an illustration. It is not a theory of snap judgements, and it must not be presented as neuroscience. Expert first impressions are sometimes accurate because experience has compressed the checking. A quick judgement may also reflect time pressure or trust rather than a skipped step.
