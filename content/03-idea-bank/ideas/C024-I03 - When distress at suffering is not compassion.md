# C024-I03 — When distress at suffering is not compassion

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Being shaken or grief-stricken by someone's suffering can look like compassion but work differently from a steady wish that the person be free of it.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source defines compassion as being moved by others' suffering, not being able to bear it, and wanting to release them from it, directed at suffering beings without dividing them into friends and enemies. It then draws two lines. First, sometimes on merely hearing or seeing another's pain, the mind becomes unsettled, sorrow arises, and a person begins to weep and lament. The source calls this a form of aversion "in the guise of compassion" and an enemy of real compassion: sorrow, it says, is a reason for weeping but not for compassion. Second, pity for one's own parents, children or relatives is treated as a kind of attachment rather than this impartial care. The tension, as I interpret it, is that the distress people feel is often taken as proof that they care, while it may be partly about their own discomfort, and care may narrow to their own circle.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: caregivers, disaster volunteers or relatives of the long-term ill describing how their early distress changed and whether it helped or hindered what they could do. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How does being overwhelmed by someone's pain differ from wanting, steadily, for it to end?

## Limits and alternatives

The claim that weeping sorrow is unwholesome aversion is a doctrinal classification, not a judgement on grieving people, and it must not be used to label anyone's tears. Calling love for family "greed" is technical language, not a devaluation of family care. The source's aggregate distinction is not psychology. Distress and steady care often coexist; distress may motivate help, and composure may be avoidance or exhaustion rather than compassion.
