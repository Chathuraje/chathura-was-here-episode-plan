# C085-I03 — Following an appetite through to its aftermath

**Status:** Held. The source practice deliberately cultivates disgust toward food, and a secular rendering could promote harmful attitudes to eating. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

A pleasure looks different when it is followed past the moment of enjoyment, through what it took to get and what becomes of it afterwards. Seeing the whole course may loosen the pull of taste without removing the need.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source asks a monk to reflect on food in ten ways. These include the dirty road to the village, begging where some give stale food and some answer with insults, chewing, digestion, waste, and washing that never quite cleans. One contrast stands out. Food is eaten in company and with delight on the first day, yet the next day what remains leaves alone and hidden, with the face turned away in shame. The stated purpose is not to stop eating. It is to eat like a starving traveller who must eat to cross a desert, for the crossing and not for the taste. My interpretation is that the practice works by lengthening the view of a pleasure until its later stages are visible. The tension is between an appetite that sees only the moment and a need that remains real.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction, pending review: how people who grow, cook, serve or clear away food describe seeing a feast differently because they know its before and after. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

Does knowing where a pleasure comes from and where it ends change how much we want it?

## Limits and alternatives

Held because the method is built on disgust and on a son's-flesh simile. It must never become dietary advice or be used with people who have eating difficulties. People's changed relation to food may instead come from health, scarcity, religious fasting or respect for labour, none of which involve disgust. Waste handling and begging must not be portrayed as degrading.
