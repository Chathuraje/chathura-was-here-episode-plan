# C047-I02 — Sensing another's pain you cannot see

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

The eye sees only another person's body and face; their pain, love, anger or jealousy is picked up by the mind, as a separate act from seeing.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source says that when you see someone suffering and their pain reaches you, what takes place is a mental process whose object is that person's feeling. It gives the same account for sensing love, anger, conceit, jealousy, kindness or compassion in others, and for recalling these states in oneself. The source classifies this; it does not say whether such sensing is accurate. The tension is my interpretation. Because the feeling is taken by the mind rather than seen, it can be close to right, or shaped by one's own memories of pain, or simply wrong. People act on what they sense in others, offering help, keeping away or taking offence, often with confidence the evidence may not support. A calm face can be misread as indifference, or silence as anger.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people whose work or care depends on reading others' distress without being told, such as carers of elderly relatives, nurses, teachers of young children or neighbours after a loss, and the times their sense proved right or mistaken. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do we come to feel another person's pain, and how would we know if we had it wrong?

## Limits and alternatives

This is a limited analogy from a classification of mental objects. The source makes no claim about accuracy, and it must not be equated with scientific theories of empathy or mirror systems. Sensing may rest on learned cues, cultural display rules or what the person has said, not on any direct grasp of feeling. A carer's response can come from duty or routine rather than sensed pain.
