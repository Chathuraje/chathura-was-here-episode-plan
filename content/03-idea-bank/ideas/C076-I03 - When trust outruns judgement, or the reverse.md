# C076-I03 — When trust outruns judgement, or the reverse

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Trust and critical judgement fail in different ways when either stands alone. Trust without judgement puts faith in the wrong things. Judgement without trust can argue itself out of doing any good at all.

## Human mechanism and tension

In explaining how to balance the mind's faculties, the source pairs faith with wisdom. Strong faith with weak wisdom makes a person trust what does not deserve it. Strong wisdom with weak faith drifts toward craftiness, which the source calls hard to cure, "like an illness caused by the medicine". Its example is someone who reasons that merely having a good intention already counts as goodness, and so never actually gives. When the two are balanced, trust goes to what deserves it. The tension, as I read it beyond the text, is that each quality is usually praised as the cure for the other's failure. Yet cleverness can be the very tool a person uses to excuse inaction, and devotion can stop them asking questions.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: situations where people decide whom or what to trust with money, care or loyalty. Examples could be contributing to a local collection or scheme, following a healer or teacher, or joining a cause. The research would explore those who trusted too readily and those who, questioning everything, stopped contributing. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do we know whether our doubts are protecting us or just excusing us?

## Limits and alternatives

In the source, faith means confidence in the Buddha, the teaching and the community. Widening it to trust in general is interpretation. The source's claim that the crafty person is reborn in hell is doctrinal and excluded. Gullibility may also come from need, isolation or deception by others. Refusing to give may be prudence, poverty or past betrayal, not rationalising. Behaviour alone cannot show which.
