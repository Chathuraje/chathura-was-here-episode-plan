# C094-I03 — When a teacher's verdict stops the learner

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

At an early sign of progress, a teacher's "yes, that's it" can make a learner stop as if finished, and "no, that's not it" can make them give up. Telling them to keep going may serve better than either verdict.

## Human mechanism and tension

The commentary says that when a sign appears, the meditator should tell the teacher what is appearing. The teacher should say neither "that is the sign" nor "that is not the sign", but "Friend, this happens to one who practises," and have them keep attending. Told it is the sign, the learner thinks the hard-won goal is reached and stops there. Told it is not, they think that after all this effort not even a sign has come, lose heart and fall back. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that learners at a threshold are highly sensitive to labels, and both approval and correction can end the effort they were meant to guide. The tension is between the need for reassurance and the risk that a firm answer replaces the learner's own looking.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: apprenticeships and training where a first partial success is hard to judge, such as learning to hear a fault in an engine, read a pulse or find balance in a dance form. The inquiry would ask teachers how they answer "is this it?", and learners how such answers affected them. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What does a learner lose when a teacher tells them they have arrived, or that they have not?

## Limits and alternatives

The source concerns one meditative sign, not teaching generally. Some learners need clear correction to avoid harmful mistakes, and withholding a verdict can feel evasive. Stopping after approval may reflect reasonable satisfaction or fatigue, and giving up after a "no" may reflect earlier discouragement rather than the teacher's word.
