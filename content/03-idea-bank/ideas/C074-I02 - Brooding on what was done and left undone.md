# C074-I02 — Brooding on what was done and left undone

**Status:** Merged into C022-I01 (*When regret keeps the wrong alive*) during consolidation on 2026-09-17. Both cards cover regret that keeps returning to what was done or left undone. C074 adds brooding over past misfortune and the loss of calm. This card keeps its ID as an alias and still holds its own source evidence. Use C022-I01 as the main card. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

A mind that keeps returning to past misfortunes, and to regret over wrongs done and good left undone, cannot settle. That restlessness blocks the calm that clear action needs.

## Human mechanism and tension

The sources pair two states. Restlessness is a mind that will not settle, like wind-rippled water or a flag that cannot stay still. Remorse is sorrow over wrongs done and good left undone, with regret as its function. Both shake the mind, both arise from thinking over past misfortunes (one source says relatives' misfortunes), and calm opposes both. Aids listed include questioning, consulting elders, good friends and suitable conversation. The tension, in my interpretation, is that regret feels like responsibility, yet endless replaying can prevent the steady attention needed to repair anything. The source does not call regret useless; it says this agitated form obstructs a settled mind.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people who keep replaying a family event, such as a death, an illness noticed late or a decision about migration, asked how they tell brooding from making amends and what has helped them settle. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When does going back over what we did or failed to do help us, and when does it only keep us from resting?

## Limits and alternatives

The classification is monastic, and one source's remorse examples concern what is allowable under monastic rules. This is not a claim about depression, anxiety or trauma, which need clinical sources. Persistent worry may be a proportionate response to real danger or unresolved injustice. Grief has its own course, and no one should be judged "brooding" from outside.
