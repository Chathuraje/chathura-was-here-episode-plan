# C076-I02 — Knowing when to push and when to leave alone

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Steady work on something difficult may need three different moves at different times: pushing, cooling down and simply watching. Any one of them done all the time spoils the work.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source uses a goldsmith's image. From time to time the smith blows on the fire, sprinkles water and just watches. If he only blows, the gold burns. If he only sprinkles, it cools. If he only watches, it never refines. Done in turn, the gold becomes pliable enough for any ornament. The source applies this to a mind in training: only settling breeds laziness, and only exertion breeds restlessness. It lists matching moves. Restrain the mind when it is over-excited, rouse it when slack, gladden it when dull, and look on when it runs evenly, like a charioteer who makes no effort while his horses run evenly. My interpretation is that the hardest part may be the last one: recognising that things are going well and not interfering.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: crafts and care work that depend on timing, such as firing pottery, tending a hearth or kiln, coaching an athlete before a race, or nursing someone through recovery. The inquiry would ask how practitioners know when to add effort, when to ease off and when to leave things alone. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do we tell when something needs more effort from us and when it needs us to stop interfering?

## Limits and alternatives

The source describes a meditator balancing mental factors toward liberation, not a general theory of work or care. The goldsmith and charioteer are its similes; craft practice is my extension. Knowing when to intervene may rest on tacit skill, external signals, instructions or luck rather than any inner balancing. Hesitating to intervene can also be avoidance rather than wise restraint.
