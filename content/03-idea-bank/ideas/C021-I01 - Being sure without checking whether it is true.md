# C021-I01 — Being sure without checking whether it is true

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

The capacity to make up one's mind is separate from the capacity to be right. A mind can settle firmly on something false, just as it can on something true.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source describes a mental factor whose work is to settle the object: to stop the mind going two or three ways and let it take the object in one definite way. It is the opposite of doubt, and without it the mind cannot properly grasp anything. But the source is explicit that this factor does not distinguish true from false or good from bad. It grips whatever is in front of it and fixes it in the mind. One source goes further and says it therefore often believes the untrue as true. The other says only that it settles “rightly or wrongly”. The tension, as I interpret it, is that people need decisiveness to act at all, yet the feeling of being settled is no evidence of being correct.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people who became firmly convinced of something, such as a rumour, a cure, a business opportunity or a judgment about a neighbour, and later found out whether it was true. The inquiry would ask what made them feel sure, and whether that feeling changed when the facts did. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What makes a conclusion feel settled before we know whether it is right?

## Limits and alternatives

The source describes a momentary mental factor in a doctrinal system, not a psychological theory of belief or bias. The two sources differ on how often this factor settles wrongly, and that difference should be preserved. Firm conviction can also come from long experience or good evidence. Someone may appear certain for social reasons, such as needing to appear strong, while privately doubting.
