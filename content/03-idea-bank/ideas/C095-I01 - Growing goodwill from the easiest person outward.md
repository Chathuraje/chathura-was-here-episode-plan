# C095-I01 — Growing goodwill from the easiest person outward

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Wishing others well is built in steps, from the person easiest to wish well toward the hardest. When anger returns, the practitioner goes back to an easier person before trying again.

## Human mechanism and tension

The sources warn against starting in the wrong place. A disliked person is tiring to hold as dear. A very close friend's smallest trouble brings lament. A neutral person is hard to feel warmly toward, and an enemy brings anger. One begins with oneself, but only as a witness: as I want happiness, so do others. Then one moves to an admired person, a dear friend, a neutral person and finally an enemy, making the mind soft and workable at each stage. If resentment flares, one returns to an easier person, settles, and tries again. The end point is tested by bandits demanding one of four people, oneself included. Only when one would hand over none is the mind equal. My interpretation is that the method treats goodwill as a capacity that can be stretched, not a fixed trait. The tension is that the hardest person is the real goal, yet starting there tends to fail.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people working toward reconciliation after a family rift or community dispute. The research would ask whether they approached the hardest relationship directly or built up to it, and what helped when anger returned. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

Can goodwill toward someone who hurt us be approached gradually, and what happens when it is forced?

## Limits and alternatives

This is a meditation sequence aimed at absorption, not a counselling method, and says nothing about whether reconciliation is wise or safe. Alternative explanations: change toward an enemy may come from time, apology or distance rather than practice. Stated goodwill may also be insincere, which Margaya says has no force.
