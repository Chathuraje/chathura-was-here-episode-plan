# C055-I01 — Trying to outweigh past harm with later good

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

People try to balance earlier harm with later good. That effort can change the weight of the past, but it may not erase it.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source says an act has no fixed weight. Later acts can strengthen it, wear it down or cut it off. One text advises that someone who has done wrong should do good that runs against it. The reverse also happens, when later harm erodes a good beginning. Related passages set a limit. A ruler who killed his father repented, sought forgiveness and gave lavishly, yet the gravest result was not prevented. A man who had killed many changed profoundly, yet blows still reached him. As I interpret it, two things are true together. Repair is worth doing and changes what follows. Some harms remain part of the lives involved, however much good comes after. The source speaks of moral results across lives. Here it is read only as a question about amends.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people who caused serious harm and then spent years giving, serving or rebuilding. The research would also listen to those who were harmed, who may judge differently whether anything has been balanced. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When someone spends years doing good after causing harm, what changes, and what stays?

## Limits and alternatives

This does not establish that good cancels harm or that misfortune repays wrongdoing, and those claims are doctrinal. Later generosity may come from remorse, but it may also come from wanting to restore a reputation, from obligation or from changed circumstances. The doer's efforts do not measure what those harmed need.
