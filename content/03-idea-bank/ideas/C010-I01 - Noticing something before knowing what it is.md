# C010-I01 — Noticing something before knowing what it is

**Status:** Merged into C034-I01 (*Seeing before knowing what you see*) during consolidation on 2026-09-17. Both cards say that turning toward something comes before knowing what it is. C010 adds the rootless functional-mind evidence. This card keeps its ID as an alias and still holds its own source evidence. Use C034-I01 as the main card. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Being drawn toward something and knowing what it is may be two different events. The mind can turn toward a sound or movement before it knows what it has turned toward.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source describes a mind-state whose only job is to turn the stream of mind toward something that has struck one of the senses. It says plainly that this state cannot know the object; knowing comes afterwards. A related state later fixes the object as "such". Both sources call this turning weak, neutral in feeling and morally neutral. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that people often treat noticing and understanding as one act, and respond as if they already know what has arrived. If the two are separate, there is a gap in which the pull has happened but the meaning is not yet settled. The source itself says nothing about reactions here; it only describes the order of functions.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: work where people must turn quickly toward uncertain signals, such as listening for trouble on a night shift or a guide reading movement in a forest. The research would ask what happens between noticing and naming. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

In the moment after something catches our attention, what decides what we take it to be?

## Limits and alternatives

The source describes single mind-moments in a doctrinal model of cognition, not a reportable experience, and defers full explanation to a later chapter. It must not be presented as attention science. Alternatively, orienting and recognition may overlap, so no experienced gap exists, and fast reactions may come from reflex or training rather than interpretation.
