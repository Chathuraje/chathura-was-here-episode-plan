# C007-I02 — Unable to bear another person's good fortune

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Seeing others eat, dress, travel, live or be honoured better than oneself can produce a painful, resisting reaction, even when their gain takes nothing from the person watching.

## Human mechanism and tension

One source defines envy as not tolerating, not being able to bear, another's prosperity and progress. It places envy in the same family as anger: a painful mind pushing against its object. The second source lists what sets it off. They are ordinary visible markers: food, clothes, vehicles, houses, respect. The first source pairs envy with stinginess, which it describes as hiding one's own goods so that they do not reach others. The tension, as I interpret it beyond the text, is that the object of resistance is someone else's comfort, which usually does the watcher no harm. The pain comes from comparison, not loss. The source also notes that people who constantly look for others' faults produce many such states in a single day, which suggests envy can become a habit of looking.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: moments when visible signs of success change how neighbours, relatives or colleagues relate. Examples might include a new house or vehicle in a village, a returning migrant worker's savings, or a relative's promotion or public honour. The inquiry would ask how people describe what they felt, without assuming envy. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

Why can someone else's good fortune feel like something taken from us?

## Limits and alternatives

The source classifies envy doctrinally as unwholesome. It does not offer a social theory. Discomfort at another's display may instead reflect a real sense of unfairness, such as wealth gained dishonestly, or worry about one's own family's security, or concern about changing community relations. An observer cannot tell envy from these by behaviour alone, and no community or group should be characterised as envious.
