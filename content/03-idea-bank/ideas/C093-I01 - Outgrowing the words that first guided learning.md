# C093-I01 — Outgrowing the words that first guided learning

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Some learning starts with words said again and again until what they point to appears clearly. Later the words can be set aside, like a trail marker nobody needs once the path is worn.

## Human mechanism and tension

The sources describe learning a list of body parts for meditation. It is recited aloud, in groups, forwards and backwards, for days, even by someone who knows the scriptures by heart. Spoken recitation supports silent recitation, which supports seeing what the list is about. Then the learner goes beyond the names. The simile is people who tie palm leaves to mark a forest well. Once their feet wear a path, they ignore the marker. Order and pace also matter. Skipping rungs exhausts a climber, rushing reaches the end without knowing the road, and dawdling never arrives. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that verbal scaffolding is necessary early and can become an obstacle later. The tension is knowing when a learner still needs the words and when holding on to them keeps attention on labels instead of the thing itself.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: skills passed on through recited sequences, such as chants, drumming patterns, craft steps, navigation cues or recipes. The research would ask practitioners when they stopped needing to say the steps and what changed. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When does saying the steps help us see, and when does it start to get in the way?

## Limits and alternatives

This is a limited analogy. The source's goal is meditative absorption on the body's repulsiveness, and its example of monks attaining stream-entry by recitation is doctrinal. It makes no claim about learning in general or about memory science. Alternative explanations: fluent performers may still silently use the words without noticing, and dropping the words may reflect automatic habit rather than deeper understanding.
