# C016-I01 — Mistaking a striking experience for arrival

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

On a long path of effort, vivid experiences along the way can feel like the goal itself. Treating them as arrival can turn a person off course.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source describes a meditator developing insight who reaches a point where ten kinds of "corruption of insight" appear, beginning with illumination. It says that when these arise, the practitioner may think "this is the path" and go down a wrong road. The next stage of purification is defined precisely as the knowledge that holds the practitioner back from that wrong road and shows the right one. What is notable is that the source calls these experiences corruptions not because they are unpleasant but because they are impressive enough to be mistaken for the result. It treats telling them apart as a skill of its own, placed after much preparation. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that the tension appears wherever people work toward something that cannot be easily checked: a striking moment offers certainty, while continuing means doubting the very experience that seemed to confirm the effort.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: how long-term practitioners, such as meditation teachers, musicians, pilgrims or people in recovery, describe early high points they once took for completion, and how they later came to see them differently. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do we know whether a powerful moment marks where we have arrived or only a place we passed through?

## Limits and alternatives

The source lists these experiences only by category and says little about them. Its "path" is a doctrinal supramundane attainment that cannot be verified here. The peak experience may in fact be meaningful or lasting for a person, and later re-evaluation may reflect changed expectations or community influence rather than better discernment. Observers cannot judge someone else's inner progress.
