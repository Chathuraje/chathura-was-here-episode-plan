# C033-I01 — What keeps the mind gripped on something

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

What brings something to mind is different from what keeps the mind fixed on it. Craving and hostility can hold attention just as firmly as generosity, goodwill and understanding.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source defines six "roots". It insists that they are not the causes that make a mind-state arise, the way fire causes smoke. Arising depends on other conditions: an ongoing stream shaped by ignorance and craving, contact with an object, and a physical basis. Roots instead do what a tree's roots do. They keep the mind standing firm on its object. Greed "bites hard" into the thing wanted, aversion bites hard into the thing resisted, and the whole mind-state stays on the object without wavering. Moments without any root are called weak and unstable. My interpretation is that steadiness of attention is morally neutral in itself. A grudge or a longing can be as steady as devotion or care. People may mistake the strength of their focus for evidence that it is worthwhile. The distinction also suggests that what began a preoccupation may not be what sustains it.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: long preoccupations of different kinds. Examples include a land boundary dispute pursued for years, a determined effort to restore a family business, and a long commitment to caring for a place or tradition. The research would ask what first drew people in and what has kept them there, and whether those turned out to be different. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When something holds our attention for years, is it the same thing that first caught it?

## Limits and alternatives

The source analyses momentary mind-states, not years-long preoccupations. Applying it across time is a limited analogy. "Root" is a technical Abhidhamma term and not a theory of attention in cognitive science. Persistence may be sustained by external factors such as legal processes, family expectation or economic necessity, not by any inner root. From outside, one cannot tell whether a person's focus comes from craving, resentment or care.
