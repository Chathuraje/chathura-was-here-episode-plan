# C100-I02 — Taking stock of what is gone and what remains

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

After a real turning point, it is possible to look back in separate ways: at what was achieved, at what it brought, at what has truly been left behind, and at what is still there.

## Human mechanism and tension

All three sources say that after each decisive breakthrough the mind reviews in turn. It reviews the way it went, the result it received, the mental stains it has abandoned, the stains that still remain, and the peace it has touched. What remains is reviewed separately from what is gone. Only someone with nothing left to abandon has four reviewings instead of five. The sources add that some people skip the review of abandoned and remaining stains altogether. My interpretation is that this separates celebrating an achievement from auditing it. After a change, a person can dwell on having arrived without checking what has actually shifted. The tension lies between the relief of a milestone and the less comfortable honesty of naming what is unfinished.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people looking back after a hard-won change, such as recovering from an addiction, completing a long training, leaving a harmful situation or rebuilding after loss. The research would ask how they tell what has truly ended from what still lingers. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

After something important has changed, how do we tell what is truly behind us from what is still with us?

## Limits and alternatives

In the source, the reviewing follows a doctrinal event in which mental stains are cut off permanently. Everyday change is gradual and reversible. People may skip stock-taking out of exhaustion, humility, cultural reticence or because the change is still fragile, not out of complacency. Self-assessment can also be distorted by optimism or harsh self-criticism.
