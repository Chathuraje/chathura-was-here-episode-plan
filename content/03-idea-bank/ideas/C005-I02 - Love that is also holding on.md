# C005-I02 — Love that is also holding on

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Much of the warmth of family life, such as providing for a spouse, pampering children or arranging their futures, can be real care and attachment at the same time.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source names occasions it considers *mostly* driven by attachment: young people seeing and talking with each other, spouses speaking affectionately, buying good clothes for a wife and children, raising and pampering children, and arranging their marriages. It does not call these wrongdoing. A related passage (C006) says attachment to spouse, children and wealth is weak in its results compared with harmful acts such as stealing. So the source holds two things together: these are ordinary, largely harmless expressions of love, and they are still forms of clinging. The tension, in my interpretation, is between giving and needing. A parent's devotion can serve the child, and it can also serve the parent's wish to keep, shape or be completed by the child. Usually it does both. The word “mostly” leaves room for care that is not clinging.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: turning points where family care is tested by letting go. Examples include a child leaving the village for work or study, a marriage being arranged or refused, or parents deciding how much to spend on a child's wedding or education. The research would listen for how people themselves describe the mix of love and holding on. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

In caring for the people we love, how can we tell what we give for them from what we hold on to for ourselves?

## Limits and alternatives

“Greed-rooted” is a technical term for attachment. In English it must not become “selfish” or be used to judge parents or spouses. The source's claim that such states are *mostly* attachment is doctrinal, not measured. Family decisions such as arranged marriages also reflect economics, culture, duty and the wishes of the young people themselves. Those factors are not explained by attachment alone.
