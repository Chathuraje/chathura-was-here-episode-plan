### 1. Summary of the Central Human Experience

The central human experience explored here is the profound disconnect between how a person perceives their immediate desires and the actual moral reality of their actions. Within everyday sensory existence, human consciousness is continuously drawn toward pleasant sensations and repelled by unpleasant ones. At the core of this movement lies a fundamental cognitive blindness—an inability to see the true nature of reality, moral cause and effect, and the long-term consequences of one's choices. 

Under the sway of this blindness, individuals do not experience reality neutrally. Instead, they divide the world into things to be grasped with craving or rejected with hostility. This dynamic is not merely an intellectual mistake; it is an active psychological condition that drives personal intentions, justifies destructive behavior, and shapes the course of an individual’s life and subsequent existences. The central issue is the tragic ease with which human beings, even when highly intelligent or formally educated, can cause severe harm to themselves and other living beings while believing they are simply pursuing legitimate comfort, pleasure, or self-preservation.

---

### 2. What a Person Commonly Believes or Misunderstands

A person undergoing this experience commonly believes that sensory gratification is the true measure of well-being. When an agreeable object, comfort, or opportunity appears, they assume that acquiring and consuming it will bring lasting satisfaction, failing to recognize the underlying craving that drives them. Conversely, when encountering an obstacle, frustration, or disagreeable condition, they assume that their anger and aggressive resistance are entirely justified reactions to an external threat. In both cases, the person mistakes their reactive impulse for rational self-interest.

Furthermore, people frequently adopt comforting misunderstandings regarding the nature of causality and personal responsibility. An individual may convince themselves that actions carry no ultimate consequences, that life ends entirely at death, or that ethical boundaries are arbitrary human inventions. Others adopt rigid dogmas, believing that an external creator or guaranteed cosmic salvation exempts them from the moral weight of their daily actions. Even those who intellectually understand right from wrong often believe that their theoretical knowledge is enough to keep them ethical, failing to realize that without deeply cultivated habits of character, immediate impulses will effortlessly override their intellectual ideals.

---

### 3. What Is Actually Happening

The underlying reality is a multi-layered psychological process that begins with perception and ends in ethical consequence. An external event or physical condition occurs, carrying an inherent quality that registers as pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. Before a person can consciously reflect, their fundamental cognitive blindness veils the reality of the situation. This blindness does not erase the senses; rather, it obscures the moral dimensions and the broader causal web surrounding the object of experience.

Once cognitive blindness obscures the situation, the mind projects an exaggerated value onto the object. If the object is perceived as agreeable, consciousness attaches to it through craving, which is experienced either with active joy and exhilaration or with calm, indifferent complacency. If the object is perceived as disagreeable, consciousness reacts with hostility, irritation, and resistance. When cognitive blindness is exceptionally dense, the mind is paralyzed by confusion, restlessness, and chronic indecision, unable to determine the true nature of what it experiences.

This process must not be confused with the idea that the external world is imaginary. Physical pain, sickness, material scarcity, and real conflict are genuine conditions. However, the psychological process described here reveals how the mind adds a layer of grasping, hostility, or rationalized justification onto those conditions, transforming a simple encounter with the world into a morally destructive intention.

---

### 4. How the Experience Develops

The experience develops in a continuous causal sequence from internal perception to outward consequence. It begins at the very dawn of an individual's existence, where the first active movement of consciousness is an instinctual attachment to its own being and continuity. Throughout life, as the individual encounters ongoing sensory stimuli, this underlying attachment meets agreeable sights, sounds, tastes, and ideas. If the mind is unreflective, attachment arises either spontaneously from past momentum or through deliberate encouragement and social prompting.

As these reactive states of craving or aversion gain strength, they translate into deliberate intentions. These intentions then express themselves through physical, verbal, and mental actions. When an individual carries out a harmful intention to completion—such as taking life, stealing, engaging in sexual exploitation, speaking deceitfully, uttering harsh words, spreading slander, gossiping, envying another’s possessions, harboring violent malice, or adhering to destructive views—they leave an indelible causal imprint upon their stream of consciousness. 

Repeatedly indulging these reactive states hardens them into automatic habits, making subsequent restraint increasingly difficult. Over time, these completed actions produce profound consequences: they determine the adverse conditions experienced throughout the individual's present life and ultimately dictate a trajectory toward intense suffering after death. Only by recognizing this causal process and deliberately cultivating stable, wholesome dispositions over time can the automatic cycle of impulse and destruction be interrupted.

### 5. How This Appears in Ordinary Life

In ordinary life, this process manifests vividly in the cultural normalization of harm for the sake of physical pleasure and comfort. For instance, the mass slaughter of animals, the destruction of developing life for food, and the pursuit of sport fishing and hunting are widely practiced and celebrated as leisure or culinary refinement. In these activities, individuals experience genuine enjoyment or complete emotional indifference, remaining entirely blind to the destruction of life they are causing.

The process also appears in commercial environments centered on sensory stimulation and risk, such as gambling and the consumption of intoxicating substances. Here, individuals chase fleeting sensations of excitement or temporary relief, actively rationalizing the financial, emotional, and social damage inflicted on themselves and their families. The desire is often supported by peers or advertising, illustrating how social prompting can stimulate and normalize destructive craving.

At an institutional and geopolitical level, this dynamic reveals itself in the scientific and technological development of weapons of mass destruction. Individuals with advanced education and technical mastery apply immense intellect to engineer tools capable of wiping out entire populations in moments. They justify their work through abstract political theories, national defense arguments, or professional duty, completely disconnected from the catastrophic moral reality of their creations.

### 6. Relevant People and Human Situations

This human experience can be found among people across various social strata, occupations, and intellectual backgrounds. A significant group includes highly specialized professionals, technical experts, and researchers who possess extensive knowledge in their fields but lack ethical self-awareness. These individuals may design exploitative economic systems or destructive machinery, completely insulated from the moral consequences of their daily labor.

Another relevant group consists of individuals engaged in extractive, exploitative, or blood-sport industries—such as commercial hunting, animal harvesting, or predatory financial ventures. These individuals often view their actions purely through the lens of economic livelihood, tradition, or recreation, demonstrating how ingrained cultural practices can sustain profound moral blindness across generations.

Finally, this experience is present in thoughtful, educated individuals who profess high ethical ideals or religious beliefs yet repeatedly succumb to destructive habits, dishonesty, or interpersonal malice. Their situation highlights the internal conflict between abstract philosophical knowledge and the raw momentum of uncultivated, impulsive desire.

### 7. Relevant Places, Traditions, Journeys and Objects

The environments that naturally reveal this human process are places where human desire, rationalized violence, and sensory consumption intersect. Industrial slaughterhouses, processing plants, and commercial fishing vessels are physical spaces where the taking of life is mechanized and reduced to routine production, stripped of any conscious recognition of harm.

Entertainment and leisure hubs—such as betting arenas, gaming halls, luxury markets dealing in exotic animal products, and recreational hunting grounds—serve as real-world spaces where sensory pleasure is directly linked with exploitation. In these environments, the celebratory joy of participants masks the destructive reality underlying the experience.

Similarly, advanced research facilities, defense manufacturing plants, and corporate boardrooms where decisions of mass exploitation are made represent modern spaces of detached intellect. The physical objects associated with these places—specialized weapons, financial ledgers, gambling tokens, hunting equipment, or luxury commodities—derive their significance from the human craving, pride, and rationalization projected onto them.

### 8. What Could Be Observed in Reality

In real life, this process becomes visible through the striking contradictions between a person’s words, beliefs, and concrete actions. Researchers might observe an individual passionately advocating for universal peace, justice, or environmental care while simultaneously participating in exploitative consumer habits, engaging in divisive workplace gossip, or treating competitors with vindictive malice. 

Another observable pattern is the presence of celebratory joy or cold detachment during the infliction of harm. In recreational hunting or high-stakes speculation, one can observe participants laughing, cheering, or remaining completely indifferent to the loss and suffering occurring as a direct result of their actions. The absence of visible distress in these moments illustrates how craving can operate alongside positive or neutral emotional states.

One might also observe the cycle of intellectual rationalization, where an individual confronted with the negative impact of their behavior immediately retreats into theoretical justifications—arguing that their actions do not matter in the grand scheme of things, that nature is inherently cruel, or that they are merely fulfilling an inevitable role. Alternatively, one might observe the visible tension of a person who acknowledges their destructive habit, experiences short-lived regret, but repeatedly relapses due to the absence of cultivated moral restraint.

### 9. Emotional Tensions and Human Questions

The deepest emotional tension within this philosophy lies in the conflict between intellectual self-image and actual moral vulnerability. A person may pride themselves on being civilized, educated, and rational, yet find themselves completely powerless against sudden surges of greed, jealousy, or rage. Another tension exists between the immediate, exhilarating pleasure of an unwholesome act and the deep, inescapable burden of causal consequence that follows it, often leaving the individual baffled as to why their pursuit of happiness consistently results in suffering.

How does a society that values education and technological brilliance continue to justify widespread, normalized destruction for the sake of comfort and sport? What causes an individual to clearly recognize the destructive nature of an action yet feel entirely compelled to carry it out? When a person constructs elaborate intellectual theories to deny personal responsibility, what underlying fears or cravings are they attempting to protect? How does human consciousness move from the initial, innocent joy of sensory experience into deeply entrenched, destructive habits of entitlement and harm?

### 10. Relationship to Similar Concepts

This concept must be distinguished from simple emotional distress, ordinary cognitive bias, or temporary mental fatigue. While general psychology often examines how stress or faulty thinking affects decision-making, the philosophy presented here specifically addresses the systemic structure of moral blindness and its inevitable causal consequences across the entire continuum of human existence.

Unlike concepts that focus purely on momentary sensory perception or external social conditioning, this philosophy emphasizes the internal moral roots—attachment, aversion, and fundamental delusion—that actively deform perception and give rise to physical, verbal, and mental actions. It does not treat unwholesome behavior as an accidental lapse in judgment, but as the natural fruit of an untrained, deluded mind interacting with a sensory world.

Furthermore, this concept serves as a comprehensive synthesis: it connects the most subtle, split-second movements of sensory awareness to large-scale human actions, such as warfare, environmental destruction, and philosophical nihilism. It establishes that large-scale human suffering is not separate from individual mental states, but is the direct, accumulated manifestation of unchecked internal cravings and rationalized blindness.

### 11. Limits and Accuracy Boundaries

When utilizing this material for documentary research, several critical boundaries must be maintained to prevent distortion of the original philosophy. First, cognitive blindness must not be reduced to a clinical psychological diagnosis, mental illness, or simple lack of academic schooling. The philosophy makes it clear that highly educated, intellectually brilliant, and socially functional individuals are just as susceptible to fundamental delusion and destructive action as anyone else.

Second, the philosophical framework must not be used to dismiss genuine external hardships—such as physical disease, poverty, social injustice, or bereavement—as mere subjective illusions. The external conditions and the pain they cause are real; the philosophy strictly concerns how the obscured mind interprets, reacts to, and multiplies suffering through craving, hostility, and harmful intentions.

Third, one must avoid the assumption that traditional lifestyles, quiet rural occupations, or slow physical crafts automatically signify the presence of moral wisdom and freedom from delusion. The presence of attachment, aversion, and blindness is universal across all sensory environments. 

Finally, researchers must remember that an individual’s external behavior, speech, or lifestyle cannot be treated as definitive proof of their internal moral state. Visible actions provide areas for thoughtful observation and inquiry, but the internal workings of consciousness, habit, and intention remain subtle, complex, and deeply rooted in causal continuity.
