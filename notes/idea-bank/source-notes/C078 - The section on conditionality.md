## Central meaning

This is the opening of the chapter on conditions. Both sources define conditions (ප්‍රත්‍යය ධර්ම) as the things that help other things arise and continue, and conditioned things (ප්‍රත්‍යයොත්පන්න, සංඛත, සංස්කාර) as what arises from them: all consciousness, mental factors and matter. Nibbāna alone is unconditioned (අසඞ්ඛත). The Buddha is said to have taught cause and effect in three ways: briefly as the four noble truths, at middle length as dependent origination (පටිච්චසමුප්පාද, twelve factors), and in full as the twenty-four conditions of the Paṭṭhāna.

## Essential distinctions

- **Brief, middle and detailed methods** (Pradeepika lines 33–37; Margaya line 7). Pradeepika adds that the middle method picks, from many causes and many effects, one principal cause and one principal effect for each link (line 35).
- **What dependent origination does not explain** (Margaya line 13). It says only that one thing arises because another is present. It does not say which particular powers or modes a cause works through; that is the Paṭṭhāna's task.
- **The framing question** (Margaya line 3): do ultimate realities arise by themselves, are they created by some powerful being, or how do they arise? The chapter answers: from many causes.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The sources agree on the three methods and the conditioned/unconditioned split. Pradeepika places the Paṭṭhāna summary from a later page (printed 138) next to the chapter opening; Margaya instead says the four truths were covered in its chapter 7 and that it will teach dependent origination first because it is shorter (line 13). No disagreement in substance.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

The claim that the Paṭṭhāna was known through omniscient knowledge (Pradeepika line 37) and that dependent origination explains the unbroken running of saṃsāra (Margaya line 11) are doctrinal. The rejection of a creator (implicit in Margaya line 3) is a doctrinal position, not a finding.

## Human meaning paragraph

The section is an orientation, not a mechanism. Its only human-facing content is the general stance that things arise from several causes rather than by themselves or by a maker's will, and that a teacher may simplify many causes into one principal cause. That second point is developed properly in C080 (Margaya line 31; Pradeepika lines 538–558), where it supports C080-I03.

## Idea decisions

- **No suitable idea.** The text is a classification of teaching methods and a definition of terms.
- Rejected candidate: *Explaining the same thing briefly, at middle length or in full.* The source describes a teaching arrangement, not a human process, and a card would be generic advice about communication.
- Rejected candidate: *Things arise from many causes, not by themselves.* Too broad here; the concrete version is in C080-I03, which lists C078 as supporting context only.
