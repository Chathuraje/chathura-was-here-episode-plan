## Central meaning

නිර්වාණය is the fourth ultimate reality. It is unconditioned (අසඞ්ඛත): not produced, not aging, not dying. It is the cessation of suffering (දුක්ඛ නිරෝධය) reached by the removal of craving and defilements without remainder. It is not made to arise but realised by supramundane path-knowledge. Its happiness is not felt happiness (වේදයිත සුඛ) but the happiness of stilling (ව්‍යුපසම සුඛ / විමුක්ති සුඛ). It has two aspects according to whether the aggregates remain (සෝපාදිසේස, living arahant; අනුපාදිසේස, after final passing). It has three names according to the mode of insight: emptiness (සුඤ්ඤත), signless (අනිමිත්ත) and desireless (අප්පණිහිත). Pradeepika also describes arrival "in five stages" through the four paths and final passing, each cutting off future rebirths.

## Essential distinctions

- **Felt happiness versus the happiness of release.** Pradeepika line 18 and lines 94–100: nibbāna's happiness cannot be experienced through body or mind as a feeling. Margaya lines 5–7 define felt happiness concretely (sights, sounds, food, vehicles, land, money, positions, honours). Release happiness is freedom from present and future troubles. Margaya argues its value from ordinary life: people sell land and mortgage the family home to be freed from an illness or trouble. Without that freedom, wealth and friends give no ease.
- **Inferential versus direct knowledge.** Pradeepika lines 24–26: අනුබෝධ ඥානය is worldly understanding built piece by piece through hearing and reflecting; ප්‍රතිවේධ ඥානය realises the truths at once. Line 70: it cannot be known merely by hearing from others; it is සාන්දෘෂ්ටික, to be seen for oneself. Line 104: a person blind from birth cannot grasp colour however it is described. Someone who once saw a little can grasp something. Margaya line 11: ordinary people can only infer that it is good from the words of those who have seen it.
- **Why wanting renews itself** (අප්පණිහිත). Margaya line 33: because conditioned things break up, however much is obtained the wish to obtain again arises. That wish is the root of all suffering. Pradeepika line 86: no lasting happiness can exist in something that arises and ceases moment by moment. Line 149: craving-aspiration (තෘෂ්ණා ප්‍රණිධිය) leads both to pleasant and to painful states.
- **Signs** (නිමිත්ත). Margaya line 29: conditioned things carry marks that let us separate "this is good, this is bad", and these marks are the ground of defilement.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The sources agree on the definition, the two elements and the three names. Pradeepika is more scholastic. It adds the four meanings (නිස්සරණ, විවේක, අසඞ්ඛත, අමත, line 34), the fire simile (fire exists but has no fixed location; line 64–68), three ways of accepting that nibbāna exists (authority, reasoning, inference from opposites; lines 110–120), the five-stage arrival (lines 151–167) and the arahant's final cognitive process (line 169). Margaya is more practical. It gives the concrete felt-happiness list, the argument from people paying for release, the simile of the person moving from a harsh region to a good one (line 15), and everyday explanations of the three names (lines 27–33).

There is a difference in how the three names are explained. Pradeepika (lines 139–149) links each to the characteristic most evident in the insight preceding the path (non-self → emptiness, impermanence → signless, suffering → desireless). Margaya (lines 27–33) explains them by what nibbāna is free of (the burdens of defilement, distinguishing marks, recurring desire). These are complementary angles rather than a contradiction. Neither source denies the other's account.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

Nibbāna itself is a doctrinal claim about an unconditioned reality and the end of rebirth. It must never be rendered as calm, relaxation, contentment or "letting go". The five-stage cutting off of future births, the arahant's final process and the claim that no being or person exists in the ultimate sense are doctrinal. The sources explicitly say it cannot be reached by reasoning or modern science (Pradeepika lines 102–108). Cards below use only the ordinary-life comparisons the sources themselves make, and each card labels itself a limited analogy.

## Human meaning paragraph

The sources explain something they say ordinary experience cannot reach by pointing to things ordinary experience does contain. People value being freed from a trouble differently from enjoying a pleasure, and will give up what brings pleasure to secure that freedom. Getting what we want does not end wanting, because what is obtained does not last, so the wish to get it again returns. And some things cannot be understood from description; those without direct experience rely on inference and on the reports of others, while even a little first-hand experience changes what a description can convey. These three comparisons are the interpretive bridge. They are not an account of nibbāna.

## Idea decisions

- **C073-I01 — When relief is worth more than pleasure.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on Margaya lines 5–7, supported by Pradeepika line 18.
- **C073-I02 — Getting what you want and wanting it again.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on Margaya line 33 and Pradeepika lines 86 and 149.
- **C073-I03 — What a description cannot give you.** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. Based on Pradeepika lines 24–26, 70, 104 and 110–120, and Margaya line 11.
- Rejected candidate: *Letting go of a hard place once life improves* (Margaya line 15). This is the source's simile for abandoning defilements, but as a human premise it risks ranking real regions as "bad places" and contradicts the common experience of attachment surviving a move. It also overlaps I01. Kept as a held question in the batch report.
- Rejected candidate: *Sorting the world into good and bad marks* (Margaya line 29). The link between distinguishing marks and defilement is too compressed to support a mechanism without substantial invention.
- Rejected candidate: *Peace that is not a feeling.* This would present the doctrinal core as a psychological state.
