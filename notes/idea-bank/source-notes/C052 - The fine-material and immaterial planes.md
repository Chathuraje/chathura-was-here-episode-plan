## Central meaning

There are sixteen fine-material (Brahma) planes, grouped by the jhāna whose result leads to rebirth there, and four immaterial planes. Together with the eleven sense-sphere planes they make thirty-one. The sources give their vertical arrangement, the meaning of their names, which persons can be found in each and their lifespans in aeons. Pradeepika then explains the units of cosmic time and gives a long narrative of how a world-system is destroyed by fire, water or wind and re-forms. Inside that narrative is a traditional account of how human society, property, crime, kingship and social groups arose (lines 176–204).

## Essential distinctions

- **Levels of jhāna mastery map to planes.** Someone who only attained the first jhāna goes to Brahmapārisajja; some mastery to Brahmapurohita; full mastery in five ways (පඤ්චවසිතා) to Mahābrahmā (Pradeepika line 55).
- **Planes by number of aggregates.** The non-percipient plane has matter only (one aggregate), the immaterial planes have mind only (four), all others have five (Margaya line 19; Pradeepika lines 67, 87).
- **Pure Abodes** are only for non-returners, who attain final release there (Pradeepika line 83; Margaya line 25).
- **Units of time.** An asaṅkhya aeon is described by emptying a one-yojana box of mustard seeds at one seed per century. Four asaṅkhya aeons make a great aeon (Pradeepika line 140).
- **The human lifespan cycle** (Pradeepika line 142). After a destruction by weapons, famine or disease, survivors who hid in caves see their dead relatives and friends, recall the results of wrongdoing, feel spiritual urgency (සංවේගය), fear wrongdoing and begin cultivating virtue. Lifespan then rises generation by generation. When people are long-lived and "intoxicated with prosperity", not perceiving ageing and death, they take up unrighteous conduct and lifespan declines again.
- **Warning before destruction** (line 160). Gods go among humans weeping and urging loving-kindness, compassion, care for parents and respect for elders.
- **The origin narrative** (lines 176–204):
  - A being "with strong desire" tastes the savoury earth out of curiosity, craving grows, and others copy (line 176).
  - The good-looking despise the ill-looking; through that pride the food loses its taste (lines 182–184).
  - People begin gathering several days' rice at once and storing it. Husks appear, cut rice no longer regrows and the rice stands in separate clumps (line 188).
  - They agree to divide the rice and set boundaries. Someone takes rice from land that is not his without asking. Unable to correct him by advice, others strike him and drive him away. Theft, reproach, lying and taking up sticks arise (line 190).
  - They agree to appoint one person to admonish, censure and expel wrongdoers. They choose the one of finest appearance who inspires confidence and has great presence, and offer him a share of rice. He is "the Great Elect" (මහා සම්මත), lord of fields and one who pleases others by righteousness (line 192).
  - Etymologies of brāhmaṇa, ajjhāyaka, vaiśya and śūdra follow (lines 194–204).
- **Destruction is linked to the roots of unwholesome action.** Fire when lust is dominant, water with hatred, wind with delusion (Pradeepika line 214).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The sources agree on the sixteen fine-material and four immaterial planes and their order. Margaya is brief and adds the one-, four- and five-aggregate classification and where noble persons do and do not live (lines 19–25). Pradeepika adds lifespans, name meanings, the aeon narrative and the origin account; Margaya has none of these for this concept. Pradeepika calls Vehapphala a fourth-jhāna plane (line 35) but says fifth-jhāna attainers are born there (line 69). This is the fourfold versus fivefold jhāna scheme, which Margaya explains in C054 (line 3). It is not a conflict.

Disagreements and suspected errors with C054 are recorded in the batch report (lifespan of Ābhassara and Subhakiṇha; number of intermediate aeons in an asaṅkhya aeon).

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

The planes, lifespans, cosmic destruction and the link between destruction and lust, hatred or delusion are doctrinal cosmology. The origin narrative is a traditional account and must not be presented as history or anthropology. The varṇa etymologies (lines 194–204), especially the gloss of śūdra as "those of cruel, blameworthy conduct", are stigmatising and must not be carried into ideas. The rise and fall of human lifespan with morality is a doctrinal claim, not a demographic finding.

## Human meaning paragraph

Read as a story people have told about themselves, the origin narrative describes processes that are recognisably human. Taking what is needed gives way to storing up. Stored goods stop renewing, so people divide what was shared, and the new boundaries create the possibility of taking from someone else. When advice cannot correct that, people agree to hand the power to judge and punish to one person, choose that person partly for appearance and presence, and pay them from the common harvest. The lifespan passage describes a second process. Catastrophe and the sight of the dead produce urgency and restraint, while long comfort lets people stop perceiving ageing and death, and restraint erodes. These are interpretations of a mythic text used as limited analogies; the source presents them as cosmic history.

## Idea decisions

- **C052-I01 — When stockpiling turns shared plenty into boundaries.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on lines 188–190.
- **C052-I02 — Handing judgement to someone everyone agrees on.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on lines 190–192.
- **C052-I03 — Why lessons learned in disaster fade in plenty.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on line 142, supported by C054 Margaya line 33. C051 Pradeepika line 142 (the human plane valued for opportunity rather than comfort) is related doctrinal context.
- Rejected candidate: *Curiosity that others copy until it becomes craving* (line 176). The consequence in the text is supernatural (loss of body light), and the desire mechanism overlaps C006.
- Rejected candidate: *Looking down on those who look worse* (lines 182–184). The consequence is magical (food losing its taste). Appearance and status are already explored in C005-I01. Recorded as a possible supporting passage if a later concept addresses conceit.
- Rejected candidate: *Warnings of catastrophe that ask for kindness* (line 160). This is a supernatural announcement with no described human reasoning.
- Rejected candidate: anything built on the varṇa etymologies. Stigmatising and historically unfounded.
