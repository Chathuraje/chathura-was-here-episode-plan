## Central meaning

Action (කර්ම), meaning the intention (චේතනා) in wholesome or unwholesome consciousness, is classified in four tetrads: by function, by order of ripening, by time of ripening and by place of ripening (Pradeepika lines 27–55; Margaya line 5). This concept explains the first tetrad, by function (කෘත්‍ය):

1. **ජනක කර්මය (generative).** Produces resultant mind and matter at rebirth and during life (Pradeepika lines 57–71; Margaya line 18).
2. **උපත්ථම්භක / උපස්තම්භක (supportive).** Sustains the result of another action or the life it produced (Pradeepika lines 73–89; Margaya lines 20–22).
3. **උපපීළක / උපපීඩක (obstructive).** Harasses that result without cutting it off, for example through illness, enemies, crop loss, loss of work, blame and accusation (Pradeepika lines 93–103; Margaya line 24).
4. **උපඝාතක / උපච්ඡේදක (destructive).** Cuts off the other action's force, or ends a life early, and may take the opening to give its own result (Pradeepika lines 105–125; Margaya lines 26 and 30).

## Essential distinctions

- **Function, not a separate kind of action.** Margaya line 28 says there is no special class of supporting, obstructing or destructive actions. Generative actions perform these functions at different times and are named for the function.
- **Acting on beings and acting on other actions.** Margaya line 32 says support, obstruction and destruction apply to other actions as well as to beings. A weak action becomes strong through later similar actions, and a strong one becomes weak through later opposite actions. Line 34 ends with advice: people who have done wrong should do good that runs against those wrongs, to weaken their results.
- **Strength decides.** Pradeepika line 125 says a strong destructive action can overcome a weak generative one, but not the reverse.
- **Farmer simile** (Pradeepika line 127). Sowing is the generative action, tending the crop is the supportive one, drought wilting it is the obstructive one and a forest fire destroying it is the destructive one.
- **Life-arc example** (Pradeepika line 129). Devadatta's good rebirth and ordination with attainments were undermined by later harmful acts and then cut off by schism.

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

- **Agreement.** Both sources give the four functions and allow each to be wholesome or unwholesome.
- **Complementary detail.** Pradeepika gives technical detail: 29 action-consciousnesses, courses of action (කර්මපථ), the difference between rebirth and life-course results, and when each function can operate (lines 59–71, 81, 97, 111). Margaya gives lay examples of obstruction (line 24) and the ranking effects on rebirth (lines 32–34).
- **Different emphasis.** Margaya says wholesome obstructive and destructive actions are rare (lines 24 and 26). Pradeepika gives several wholesome destructive examples, such as a gift of cloth displacing a gift of food, or a monastery gift displacing a killing (lines 113–115). This is a difference of emphasis, not a direct contradiction.
- **Different definition of the destructive function.** Margaya centres it on ending a being's life before its natural span (line 26), and some such actions give no result of their own (line 30). Pradeepika centres it on cancelling another action's result-force and taking its place (line 107). Both appear, but they emphasise different things.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

Almost all of this concept is rebirth mechanics: which action gives the next birth, animal and hell births, and wealthy or poor families. Illness, enemies, madness, lost work and early death are presented as results of past action (Pradeepika line 99; Margaya lines 24 and 26). This must never become a premise that a real person's misfortune was earned. The examples of beings born as oxen or royal elephants, and the story of the hell fires being quenched (Pradeepika lines 85, 101, 103), are doctrinal narrative.

## Human meaning paragraph

Read as a limited analogy, the concept says an act does not carry a fixed weight on its own. What is done later can reinforce it, wear it down or cut it off. Margaya turns this into a practical instruction: someone who has done harm should deliberately do good that runs against it. The same logic works the other way, since later harm can erode earlier good. The inference beyond the text is that people often live as though later conduct can rebalance earlier conduct, and that this hope has limits. The related concept C056 states one such limit directly.

## Idea decisions

- **C055-I01 — Trying to outweigh past harm with later good.** Accepted as a limited analogy. Based on Margaya lines 32–34 and Pradeepika lines 125–129, with its limit taken from C056 (Pradeepika lines 53–59) and C057 (Pradeepika line 121).
- Rejected candidate: *A good start undone by what follows* (Devadatta arc, farmer simile). It is the reverse direction of the same mechanism, so it is folded into C055-I01 rather than made a separate card.
- Rejected candidate: *Hardships that wear a life down without ending it* (obstructive action). The source's examples (illness, enemies, lost work, accusations) are offered as the ripening of past action. A card would invite reading real hardship as deserved.
- Rejected candidate: the farmer simile as a general "seed, tending, drought, fire" model of outcomes. It is a generic causal picture with no specific human tension beyond I01.
