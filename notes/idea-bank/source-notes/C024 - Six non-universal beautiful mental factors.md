## Central meaning

Six wholesome mental factors do not arise in every wholesome mind-state. They are the three abstinences (විරති: සම්මා වාචා, සම්මා කම්මන්ත, සම්මා ආජීව), the two "illimitables" treated here (අප්පමඤ්ඤා: කරුණා, මුදිතා), and wisdom (පඤ්ඤින්ද්‍රිය / අමෝහ). Loving-kindness and equanimity are omitted because they are already counted as අදෝස and තත්‍රමජ්ඣත්තතා among the universal beautiful factors (Pradeepika line 77; Margaya line 29).

## Essential distinctions

- **Abstinence needs an actual object to refrain from** (Pradeepika lines 25–27, 33, 37). Speaking kindly, reading religious texts, praising others or doing honest trade are meritorious but are *not* abstinence. They are called වොහාර or චෙතනා සම්මාවාචා / සම්මාකම්මන්ත.
- **Right livelihood** (Pradeepika lines 45–51; Margaya line 9) is refraining from bodily or verbal wrongdoing done *for the sake of a living*. Examples of wrong livelihood: feigning virtue or spiritual attainment for gain, flattering people for profit, lending to someone in trouble at unjust interest in order to seize their land, endangering others for a small profit. Honest trade in itself is not this abstinence.
- **Three modes of abstinence** (Pradeepika lines 57–67; Margaya line 27). සම්පත්ත විරති: refraining when the occasion arrives, without a prior vow, by considering one's standing, age, family, learning, or the wrong itself. Story of Cakkana, who freed a trapped doe after seeing her fawns and thinking of his own sick mother. සමාදාන විරති: refraining because of precepts already undertaken. Story of a man wrapped by a python who threw away his axe because while it was in his hand the intention to kill kept arising. සමුච්ඡේද විරති: abstinence of a noble person whose defilements are uprooted (story of the cook Tissa).
- **Lawyer example** (Pradeepika line 69). A lawyer accepts a bribe to win a field for a dishonest tenant, then reconsiders and returns it. Refusing the lie is සම්මාවාචා; refusing to earn a living by lies is සම්මා ආජීව.
- **Compassion** (Pradeepika lines 81–87; Margaya line 15). Compassion takes suffering beings as object without distinction of friend or enemy. Pity for one's own parents, spouse, children and relatives is "a special form of greed", not compassion. Sorrow that arises on seeing suffering and makes one weep and lament is "a form of aversion appearing in the guise of compassion", unwholesome, an enemy of the one developing compassion. Sorrow belongs to the feeling aggregate; compassion to the formations aggregate. Margaya grounds compassion in self-comparison: not wanting others to suffer just as one does not want suffering oneself.
- **Gladness** (මුදිතා) (Pradeepika line 91; Margaya line 17) is opposed to envy. Joy at the success of one's own relatives and friends is again called a form of greed.
- **Wisdom** (Pradeepika lines 95–123; Margaya line 21) knows things as they are. Pradeepika distinguishes consciousness, perception and wisdom (line 105), requires balance of faith with wisdom and effort with concentration (line 111), lists habits that help understanding grow (line 117), and insists that skill and sharpness in wrongdoing is not wisdom; many highly learned people hold wrong views (lines 119–123).

## Agreement, complementary detail and disagreement

The sources agree on the six factors and on the definitions. Pradeepika supplies all the stories, the livelihood examples, the near-enemy analysis of compassion and the wisdom discussion; Margaya is a short summary plus memorisation lists (lines 31–45). One genuine difference: Margaya (line 27) defines සම්පත්ත විරති as including someone who *has* taken precepts but refrains without regard to them, and defines සමුච්ඡේද විරති simply as abstinence arising with supramundane path consciousness. Pradeepika (line 61) defines සම්පත්ත විරති as refraining *without* prior precepts. These are compatible but not identical. A second difference of emphasis: Margaya grounds compassion in comparison with one's own wish to avoid suffering; Pradeepika stresses impartiality and excludes compassion limited to one's own people.

## What cannot safely be carried into everyday language

The miraculous cure in the Cakkana story (an act of truth heals the mother), the python releasing the man, fear of hell as a motive (lines 33, 41, 69), and the path-based uprooting of defilements are doctrinal or legendary. The claims that wisdom "seeds" come from previous lives and that animals cannot develop wisdom (lines 103, 109) are doctrinal. "Compassion for one's family is greed" is a technical classification, not a verdict on family love. The feeling/formations aggregate distinction must not be presented as psychology.

## Human meaning paragraph

Holding back from harm is a distinct event that only occurs when harm is actually possible. It can come from the moment itself, where a person weighs who they are or suddenly sees another's situation as like their own, or from a promise made earlier that they refuse to break even at great cost. Holding back is especially tested when the wrong would feed a household. Separately, being upset by someone's suffering is not the same as wishing them free of it: distress can be about one's own discomfort, and care limited to one's own circle may be closer to attachment. These readings are my interpretation; the source frames them as mental factors, not as a psychological sequence.

## Idea decisions

- **C024-I01 — Holding back from harm, with or without a promise.** Accepted. Based on the three modes of abstinence (Pradeepika lines 57–67, Margaya line 27), using only the first two modes. Partly overlaps C023-I01 (self-respect or reputation); kept separate because its axis is in-the-moment response versus prior commitment.
- **C024-I02 — Refusing a wrong that would pay the bills.** Accepted. Based on right livelihood (Pradeepika lines 45–53, 69; Margaya line 9).
- **C024-I03 — When distress at suffering is not compassion.** Accepted. Based on Pradeepika lines 81–87 and Margaya line 15.
- Rejected candidate: *Being glad for a stranger's good fortune* (mudita versus envy and partial joy). The source gives only a definition and the family-joy exclusion; the partiality point is already carried in C024-I03. Suitable to revisit with a concept dedicated to envy.
- Rejected candidate: *Cleverness at harm is not wisdom* (lines 119–123). A real distinction, but the source's argument depends on a definitional claim that a wholesome mind can never produce harm; it becomes a moral slogan rather than a mechanism. Held as a note for any later concept on wisdom.
- Rejected candidate: balance of faith and understanding (line 111). A list with one-line consequences; better handled under a concept on the faculties.
