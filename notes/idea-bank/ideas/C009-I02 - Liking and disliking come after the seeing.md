# C009-I02 — Liking and disliking come after the seeing

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

The first contact with a sight or sound carries no liking or disliking. Liking or disliking arises afterwards, and the same thing can draw opposite responses from different people or senses.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source describes seeing as a contact so soft, like cotton touching cotton, that it cannot itself be pleasant or painful. The object is first received, as if taken into the hand, then examined. Only after it has been properly recognised does a pleased or displeased response follow. Touch is the exception: it strikes hard and brings pleasure or pain directly. Responses also vary. A sword is pleasing to look at but painful to the body. The same filth draws disgust from an ordinary person, appetite from a pig, sober reflection from a wise person. Perception can also invert, so the agreeable seems disagreeable. The tension, as I interpret it beyond the text, is that people experience their likes and dislikes as properties of things, while the source places them in a later response that depends on who is meeting the thing.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: work where outsiders' first reactions differ sharply from insiders', such as fish markets, tanneries, waste collection, or traditional medicine preparation. The inquiry would ask how the workers' responses to smells, sights or textures changed over time. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How much of our disgust or delight belongs to the thing, and how much to us?

## Limits and alternatives

The source also holds that agreeable and disagreeable have a common standard and that meeting them follows past action. Both are doctrinal and not carried here. Its sequence is a model of momentary mind-states, not experimental psychology. Some reactions may be widely shared for biological reasons. Changed responses may come from habituation, necessity or pride in work rather than a different perception.
