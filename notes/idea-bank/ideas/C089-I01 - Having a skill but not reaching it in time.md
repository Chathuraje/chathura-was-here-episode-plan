# C089-I01 — Having a skill but not reaching it in time

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Doing something with time to prepare differs from doing it at once when a threat arrives. Using that speed to protect others is rarer still.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source describes mastery as a ladder, each rung reached by only "one in hundreds of thousands": learning the method, mastering it in many orders, entering the required state quickly, and, rarest, using it to shield others in sudden danger. In one story, monks who could perform every feat step by step were not allowed to face a danger, because they could not reach the steady state fast enough when it struck. An elder facing a threat to a crowd acted "before blinking". A teacher urged students to keep their training ready, like soldiers with rust-free weapons. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that a skill needing calm conditions can fail exactly when it matters, so readiness is a separate achievement.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people whose work demands instant response on behalf of others, such as lifeguards, fishing crews, midwives, fire and rescue volunteers or bus drivers. The inquiry would ask how they describe the gap between knowing what to do and doing it in the first seconds. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What turns something we know how to do into something we can do the moment someone needs it?

## Limits and alternatives

The feats are doctrinal powers and are not claimed here; only the graded structure is used, as analogy. Speed under pressure may depend on temperament, rehearsal of specific scenarios, fitness, equipment or teamwork rather than depth of mastery. Freezing in an emergency can be a normal stress response, not a lack of skill, and must not be read as failure of character.
