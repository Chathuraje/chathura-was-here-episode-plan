# C095-I03 — Stepping back as care, not as indifference

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited application. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Care can take the form of stepping back when someone is managing their own life. That is different from not caring, even though the two can look the same from outside.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source illustrates four kinds of care with a mother of four sons. For the infant she wants growth. For the sick son she wants recovery. For the young son in his prime she wants that prime to last. For the son busy with his own affairs she does not fuss and stays uninvolved. That last attitude is equanimity: seeing all alike, calming both resistance and clinging. It fails when it becomes ignorant, worldly indifference. Its basis is recognising that nobody's wishing produces another person's happiness. Margaya is blunt: wishing people well does not make them well, so worrying over the world is fruitless effort. My interpretation is that the passage names a hard stage of care, when helping becomes interference. The tension is telling a respectful step back apart from withdrawal. Both look like doing nothing.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: parents of adult children who have left home, teachers or mentors whose students now work independently, or carers whose patient has recovered. The research would ask how they decided to stop intervening and whether others read it as coldness. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do we know when stepping back is still a way of caring?

## Limits and alternatives

The source grounds equanimity in the doctrine that beings receive happiness and suffering according to their own past actions, which in everyday use could imply people deserve their suffering. This card does not carry that basis. The source also treats this as a meditative absorption, not a parenting ideal. Alternative explanations: stepping back may reflect exhaustion, conflict avoidance or the other person asking for distance.
