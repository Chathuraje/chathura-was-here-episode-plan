# C036-I01 — Seeing through the marks we already know

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

What the senses receive is a raw blend. Knowing *what* we are looking at or hearing depends on marks we have met and grown used to before.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source says the eye never meets a "thing". It meets only a mixture of colour. A person then recognises the mixture "by marks seen and grown used to before" and says they saw such-and-such. Sounds work the same way: a drum, a lute, a human voice or thunder is identified because it was heard before. Recognition also takes further mental work after the first act of seeing. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that recognition is partly memory. Familiar marks make the world quick to read, but they can also make a person see what they expect, or fail to name something they have never met. The tension lies between the speed that experience gives and the openness that a new or changed thing demands.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people whose work or life depends on reading faint or mixed signals, such as telling ripe fruit, a changing sky, an engine sound or a bird call apart, and the difference between how an experienced person and a newcomer describe the same scene. The inquiry would ask what they say they rely on. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When we recognise something at a glance, how much of it are we seeing and how much are we remembering?

## Limits and alternatives

The source's account of colour and sound rests on doctrinal material theory and a doctrinal unit of time; it must not be presented as vision science. The source does not discuss misperception or expectation; that part is my extension. Quick recognition may equally reflect trained skill, attention or context rather than memory alone, and people cannot reliably report how they recognise things.
