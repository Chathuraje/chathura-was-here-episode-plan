# C096-I02 — Fitting an explanation to where someone is confused

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

The same understanding can be taught in different shapes. Which helps depends on what a learner is confused about, and whether they need it short or detailed.

## Human mechanism and tension

The commentary explains why the Buddha divided experience in several ways. For people confused about the mental side, the analysis gave four divisions of mind and one of body. For those confused about the physical side, it gave eleven divisions of matter. For those confused about both, it divided both in detail. The same three forms are matched to sharp, middling and slower faculties, and to people who prefer brief, medium or detailed accounts. All lead finally to the same four-part understanding. The mechanism, as I read it beyond the text, is that a good explanation expands the part the learner is confused about and compresses what they already grasp. Confusion is specific, not general. Yet teachers and institutions usually have one standard explanation, while learners arrive confused in different places.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people who explain the same thing to very different listeners, such as agricultural extension officers, pharmacists explaining medicines, temple or church teachers of mixed-age classes, or guides at a heritage site. The inquiry would ask how they notice where someone is confused, and how their explanation changes in length and emphasis. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do we find out which part of something another person does not yet understand?

## Limits and alternatives

The source concerns the Buddha's teaching and a doctrinal analysis of experience; carrying it into ordinary teaching is interpretation. Its ranking of people by "sharp" and "dull" faculties must not be applied to real learners. Learners may not know where they are confused, a preference for brevity may reflect time rather than need, and tailoring can leave some people with a thinner version.
