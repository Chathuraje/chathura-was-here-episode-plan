# C006-I03 — Needing a push to do what you already want

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

A desire may not be enough to act on by itself. Sometimes the hesitant mind needs encouragement from another person, or from the person talking themselves into it.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source separates states that arise spontaneously and keenly from those that arise only with prompting, urging or encouragement. One commentary names two sources of that push: someone else's urging, or the person deliberately rousing their own mind when it is holding back. The other commentary notes that prompted states can carry sluggishness or heaviness. So the want is real, but something in the person is reluctant. The tension I draw from this (interpretation) is that the same act can be done eagerly or half-heartedly, and hesitation does not automatically protect a person from acting. The encourager may be a friend, a group, or the person's own inner voice. The source also ranks unprompted states as stronger than prompted ones. That ranking concerns karmic result, so it should not be read as a measure of guilt.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: moments when people describe being “talked into” something, or talking themselves into it, after holding back. Group dares, a first risky purchase, or joining a practice they had doubts about are possible areas. The same research could look at people who were urged and still declined. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When we hesitate and then go ahead, whose voice finally tips the balance?

## Limits and alternatives

The source discusses prompting within greed-rooted states. Elsewhere, the same prompted/unprompted distinction also applies to wholesome states, so hesitation-then-action is not always about unwholesome desire. Reluctance may reflect fatigue, fear, conscience or practical doubt. External urging may be coercion rather than encouragement, and that carries different ethical weight. A person's later account of being “pushed” may shift responsibility in ways that need careful handling.
