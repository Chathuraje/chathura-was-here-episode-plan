# C013-I01 — Looking long enough for something to become clear

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

A thing reveals its detail only to attention that stays with it and keeps returning to it. A mind that moves from one attractive object to the next sees none of them well.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source explains one meaning of its word for absorption as close, sustained looking. Its comparison is a person who wants to examine an object properly: they keep it near, turn from other things, look long and from many angles, and every colour and marking becomes clear. The untrained mind, by contrast, takes up one sense object after another, never settled. A second source adds that ordinary focus is weak, and that attention is held by repeatedly sending the mind back each time it shrinks away. The tension, in my interpretation, is between the pull of the next interesting thing and the slower reward of staying. Clarity here is not a sudden insight but what accumulates when looking is not interrupted. The source's aim is meditative absorption, not skill in a trade.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: work that depends on long, returning attention to one object, such as restoring a mural, reading a patient's pulse, grading tea leaves, tracking birds or repairing an old instrument. The inquiry would ask what practitioners see after long looking that a glance misses. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What do we only notice once we have stopped looking for something else?

## Limits and alternatives

This is a limited application of a definition used for meditation. The source makes no claim about expertise or perception science, and Margaya's claim that absorption can last a whole day is doctrinal, not measured. Clear perception after long looking may come from accumulated knowledge, training or tools rather than from sustained attention itself. Some discoveries come from broad, wandering attention, which the source does not consider.
