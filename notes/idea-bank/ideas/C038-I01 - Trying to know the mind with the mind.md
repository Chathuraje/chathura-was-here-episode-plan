# C038-I01 — Trying to know the mind with the mind

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

The tool a person uses to understand their own mind is that same mind, so self-knowledge is always indirect: a matter of inference rather than plain sight.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source gives a simple image. A knife can cut other things but cannot cut itself. In the same way, the mind, which is the instrument of knowing, cannot directly know its own workings. It cannot be seen, or felt on the skin like wind or cold, and it changes too quickly to examine. Most people never notice how their minds work. Even those who study the mind reach only inferred knowledge, not direct perception. Yet the author urges the reader to try, because such knowledge is valuable. The tension, as I read it, is between a real need to understand oneself and the fact that every account of one's own mind is produced by the thing being described.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: situations where people are asked to explain why they acted or felt as they did, such as after a major decision, in recovery from a habit, or in reflecting on a family conflict years later, and how their explanations shift over time. The inquiry would look at how people build and revise their accounts of themselves. No person, event or outcome is established.

## Open human question

If we can only infer what happens in our own minds, what makes one account of ourselves more trustworthy than another?

## Limits and alternatives

This is the author's teaching image, not a finding from psychology, and the claim that mind-states are too fast to examine is doctrinal. Other people's observations, long practice, or writing and conversation may give partial outside views that the knife image does not consider. People may also be quite accurate about some inner states, such as pain, even if not about their causes.
