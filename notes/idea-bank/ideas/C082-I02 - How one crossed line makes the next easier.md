# C082-I02 — How one crossed line makes the next easier

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Once a person has done serious harm, restraint elsewhere can loosen too. A wrong already done becomes a strong support for further wrongs, of the same kind and of other kinds.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source says that after someone has taken a life, their mind becomes deeply unsettled and unrestrained. That unrestrained state strongly supports doing it again. It also makes taking what is not given, lying, slander, harsh speech, covetousness, ill will and wrong views "very easy". Each of these ten wrongs is described as a strong support for each of the others. For grave acts it adds another path: seeing someone else do it and deciding it is acceptable. As I interpret it, beyond the text, the lines a person keeps are linked, so breaching one weakens the others. The tension is that people usually judge each act as a separate choice, while the source treats every act as a condition for the next. Yet the source also says harm done can push a person toward good conduct.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: accounts from people in rehabilitation, restorative-justice or recovery settings who describe how a first act changed what felt possible. Other directions are workplaces where small dishonesty grew, and groups where seeing others do something made it seem normal. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

After a first line is crossed, what makes the next one look smaller, and what makes someone stop?

## Limits and alternatives

The source's illustrations include killing a parent and splitting the monastic community, with fixed rebirth results. These are doctrinal and not carried over. Escalation may instead come from opportunity, peer pressure, debt, addiction, coercion, or the need to cover up the first act. Many people stop after one wrong. No documentary subject should be framed as on a slope toward harm.
