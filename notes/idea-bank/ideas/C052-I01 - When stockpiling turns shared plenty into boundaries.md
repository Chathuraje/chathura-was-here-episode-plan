# C052-I01 — When stockpiling turns shared plenty into boundaries

**Status:** Accepted for research as a limited analogy. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

When people move from taking what they need to storing up, something held in common can start to seem scarce. It gets divided, and division makes it possible to take what is someone else's.

## Human mechanism and tension

In the source's traditional story of how society began, rice was gathered as needed and grew back. Then people began storing several days' worth. The rice stopped regrowing and stood in separate clumps. The people agreed to divide it with boundaries. Soon someone took rice from a plot that was not his. Advice did not stop him, so others struck him and drove him out, and theft, blame, lying and violence followed. The order matters: storing, then scarcity, then boundaries, then theft. My interpretation, beyond the text, is that each step feels reasonable to those taking it. Storing seems prudent and dividing seems fair, yet together they change how neighbours see each other.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: shared resources that came to be divided or fenced, such as common grazing land, a village tank, fishing grounds or inherited paddy land. Research would ask what people remember about when sharing gave way to allocation, and whether disputes followed. No person, place, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When does saving for tomorrow start to change how we treat what we once shared?

## Limits and alternatives

The source presents a myth, with magical elements, as cosmic history. It does not show that stockpiling causes theft. Division of common resources often follows population growth, state policy or real scarcity rather than changed habits. Boundaries can also reduce conflict, and storing food is often a sensible answer to uncertainty.
