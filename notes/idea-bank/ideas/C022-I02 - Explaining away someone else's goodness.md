# C022-I02 — Explaining away someone else's goodness

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Discomfort at another person's success or good standing can show up as a story about them. Their generosity or discipline becomes, in the telling, a show put on for gain.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source defines envy as being unable to bear other people's prosperity, fame or progress. Unlike stinginess, which guards one's own possessions, it looks outward. The envious mind is pleased by others' misfortune and belittles those enjoying good fortune. When it sees people giving, keeping moral discipline or meditating, it erases those qualities, presenting their conduct as hypocrisy or a technique for winning gifts and respect. The source adds that this often happens without the person realising it. The human tension, in my reading, is that the suspicion can feel like shrewdness or honesty ("I see through them"). That makes it hard to tell apart from the fair scepticism that communities also need.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: situations where someone's public generosity, a donation, a new house or a promotion draws quiet talk about hidden motives. The research would look at how neighbours, colleagues or families decide whose goodness is genuine. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

How do people decide whether someone's good deed is sincere, and what do they feel while deciding?

## Limits and alternatives

Some public goodness really is self-serving, and questioning motives can be a healthy check on power, patronage or charity used for status. Suspicion may also come from past betrayal, political conflict or real inequality rather than envy. The source classifies envy as an unwholesome factor. This card must not be used to label any documentary subject envious, since inner motives cannot be read from gossip or criticism.
