# C076-I01 — Wanting to be worth what others give up

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

Learning that someone goes without so that you can be supported can turn into a strong reason to make your own effort worth their sacrifice.

## Human mechanism and tension

Among the conditions that rouse energy, the source asks a monk to reflect on the food he receives. His donors are neither relatives nor servants. They give hoping it will bear fruit, and the support is allowed so that he can practise, not live comfortably. A lazy recipient does not honour it. In the source's story, a woman who looks after an elder "like a son" has him served good rice with milk, ghee and honey, while she plans a sour gruel of broken rice and boiled leaves for herself. The elder hears of it. He decides he cannot eat such food with a careless mind and strives before accepting it. The tension, in my interpretation, is between gratitude as motivation and gratitude as a weight. Knowing the price someone paid can focus effort. It could also become pressure or guilt, which the source does not discuss.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: students, trainees or migrant workers whose studies or start in life were paid for by a parent, relative or community eating less or working longer. The research would ask how knowing that cost shaped their effort, and when it helped or hurt. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What happens to our effort when we learn what someone else went without for us?

## Limits and alternatives

The source's setting is monastic alms and a goal of liberation. The elder's attainment is a doctrinal claim. Donors in the source give for spiritual merit, which differs from family obligation. Such effort may also come from fear of disappointing others, social expectation or ambition. Some people react to such knowledge with resentment or paralysis rather than resolve.
