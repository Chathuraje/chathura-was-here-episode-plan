# C024-I01 — Holding back from harm, with or without a promise

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

People stop themselves from harm in at least two ways: by responding to the moment when the chance arrives, or by holding to an earlier commitment even at a cost.

## Human mechanism and tension

The source separates restraint that arises when the chance to do wrong appears, with no earlier vow, from restraint rooted in a promise already taken. In the first, a person weighs the wrongness of the act, or their standing and upbringing, when the moment arrives. Its example is a man who traps a doe for his sick mother's medicine, sees her fawns, imagines his own family's grief, and frees her. In the second, a man bound by precepts is seized by a python; he decides breaking his promise would be worse than dying, and throws away his axe because while it stays in his hand the intention to kill keeps returning. My interpretation is that the first depends on seeing clearly in the moment, here by recognising another's situation as like one's own, while the second can hold when the moment is overwhelming. The axe also suggests an ordinary practice: removing the means when the wish keeps coming back.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people describing a moment when they could have taken, hurt or cheated and did not, and whether they were thinking of the other person, of who they are, or of an earlier promise. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

When we stop ourselves, is it what we see in that moment or what we promised before that holds us?

## Limits and alternatives

The stories are traditional illustrations involving a miraculous cure and fear of hell; they are doctrinal, not evidence. A third mode, restraint of one whose defilements are uprooted, is not used here. People may also refrain from fear of being caught or habit, and later accounts of why may be reconstructions.
