# C001-I02 — Learning for a title instead of for change

**Status:** Accepted for research. This is a conceptual interpretation. No real story has been verified.

## Core idea

People can study something meant to change how they live, but pursue it for recognition, positions or honour. The learning then grows while the change it promised does not.

## Human mechanism and tension

The commentary criticises several ways of engaging with the teaching that fall short. One is reading its history and life stories and feeling satisfied. Another is deciding that deep teaching is unnecessary as long as one does no harm. A third is doing valuable public service (caring for the sick, protecting the helpless, village development, publishing) without understanding how such actions become truly fruitful. The sharpest case is intelligent people who study the teaching while hoping for gain, respect, degrees and titles, instead of working to develop good qualities and give up defilements. They do not get the proper result. The mechanism is that the purpose steering the study decides what it produces. The tension, as I read it (interpretation), is that recognition is real and motivating, and institutions reward it. Yet the thing being studied points somewhere else. Knowledge and transformation can separate without anyone noticing.

## Possible everyday expression

Hypothetical research direction: people in any field of learning where credentials and inner change can separate. Examples include religious or traditional knowledge exams, professional qualifications in caring work, or apprenticeships in crafts with a moral dimension. The research would ask what the learning changed in them, apart from what it earned. No person, event or outcome has been established.

## Open human question

What does learning change in us when no one is watching or awarding it?

## Limits and alternatives

The source makes this critique within a religious framework whose goal is liberation. Its judgement of “proper result” is doctrinal. Seeking qualifications is often a necessity for livelihood, not vanity, and recognition and sincere change can coexist. The source's dismissal of social service without doctrinal understanding is its own position and should not be applied to real volunteers.
