### A Philosophical Account of the Eight Universal Wholesome States of Consciousness

---

#### 1. The Nature of Wholesome Consciousness and the "Universally Broad" Wholesome
A wholesome state of consciousness is an occurrence within a person’s mental continuum that suppresses the destructive roots of greed, aversion, and delusion, eradicates harmful tendencies, and produces agreeable, beneficial consequences in the future. Because it counteracts and destroys corrupting mental states, it is designated as wholesome. Such states carry the capacity to direct the continuum toward favorable future existences (such as human or celestial realms) and generate experiences of well-being and material fulfillment.

In the course of ordinary life, these states arise through ethical actions such as generosity, moral discipline, mental cultivation, paying reverence, offering service, sharing goodness, rejoicing in others' goodness, listening to truth, explaining truth, and rectifying one's perspective. 

These everyday ethical states are categorized as "universally broad" (or "great") wholesome states not because they surpass higher meditative absorptions or transcendent states of liberation, but because they are accessible and experienced by the vast majority of people everywhere. Higher meditative and transcendent states occur only rarely and to a few, whereas these baseline wholesome states form the universal foundation of everyday moral life.

---

#### 2. The Three Determining Dimensions of Wholesome Consciousness
Although the fundamental wholesome impulse is singular, it is differentiated into eight distinct states based on three operational factors:

1. **The Affective Dimension (Feeling):**
   * **Accompanied by Joy:** The wholesome act is carried out with a feeling of positive pleasure or joy. This occurs due to specific conditions: strong conviction, the availability of suitable gifts or objects, encountering virtuous and worthy recipients, favorable time and place, the support of good friends, a pure worldview, understanding the benefits of giving, being born with an innate disposition characterized by joy, and freedom from physical illness.
   * **Accompanied by Equanimity (Neutrality):** When these joyful conditions are diminished, lacking, or when the object of action is met with an emotionally neutral disposition, the action takes place in an emotionally balanced, neutral state without active delight.

2. **The Epistemic Dimension (Understanding):**
   * **Endowed with Insight:** The action is accompanied by an understanding of moral causation (the reality of actions and their consequences), causal relations, the true meaning of moral teaching, and the practical means of abstaining from harm. This insight is not mundane cleverness or worldly skill (which can be applied destructively, such as in killing living beings or manufacturing weapons and poisons). Genuine wholesome insight recognizes good and evil, abstains from harm, promotes mutual well-being, seeks release from the suffering of repeated existence, and aims for excellence in moral character as well as intellect. Its arising is supported by an initial birth-consciousness endowed with the three positive roots (non-greed, non-aversion, non-delusion), the maturation of virtues such as conviction, detachment from unwholesome deeds, and engaging in activities that cultivate wisdom.
   * **Devoid of Insight:** The action is performed without understanding moral causation, occurring purely out of habit or social conditioning. A clear human example is that of young children who, lacking comprehension of cause and effect, merely follow their parents to a place of worship, recite verses, bow in reverence, and offer gifts.

3. **The Volitional Dynamic (Initiation and Prompting):**
   * **Unprompted (Spontaneous):** The state of mind arises spontaneously through one's own immediate initiative, without requiring self-coaxing, internal struggle, or external urging.
   * **Prompted (Induced):** The state of mind arises only after overcoming personal hesitation or as a result of the encouragement, persuasion, or directives of others.

---

#### 3. The Eight States of Wholesome Consciousness
The combination of these three factors produces eight distinct states:

1. **Joyful, endowed with insight, and unprompted:** Performing an ethical act with inner joy, clear understanding of moral cause and effect, and entirely through spontaneous personal initiative.
2. **Joyful, endowed with insight, and prompted:** Performing an ethical act with joy and understanding, but only after overcoming personal reluctance or being encouraged by others.
3. **Joyful, devoid of insight, and unprompted:** Performing an ethical act with joy and spontaneity, but purely out of routine or habit, without reflective understanding of moral causation.
4. **Joyful, devoid of insight, and prompted:** Performing an ethical act with joy, but without moral insight, and only because of external persuasion or direction.
5. **Equanimous, endowed with insight, and unprompted:** Performing an ethical act with a neutral, balanced emotional state and clear insight, through spontaneous initiative.
6. **Equanimous, endowed with insight, and prompted:** Performing an ethical act with an emotionally neutral state and clear insight, but requiring internal effort or external urging.
7. **Equanimous, devoid of insight, and unprompted:** Performing an ethical act with neither emotional joy nor insight, acting spontaneously out of mere routine.
8. **Equanimous, devoid of insight, and prompted:** Performing an ethical act with an emotionally neutral state, lacking insight, and solely because of the prompting or command of others.

---

#### 4. The Ten Moral Actions, Volition, and the Eightyfold Classification
Wholesome consciousness operates across ten specific domains of action: generosity, moral restraint, contemplation, honoring the worthy, offering service, sharing merit, rejoicing in the merit of others, listening to truth, teaching truth, and rectifying one's perspective.

Volition (intention) is the primary mental factor that directs and shapes consciousness. When each of the eight states of wholesome consciousness is combined with the intentionality of these ten moral actions (for example: a joyful, insight-endowed, unprompted act of generosity; a joyful, insight-endowed, unprompted act of moral restraint; and so on), the system expands into **eighty distinct wholesome states**.

The moral efficacy and resultant power of an act depend directly on the strength of this volition. For example, if a person gives a minor sum (such as one cent) with a joyful, insight-endowed, and unprompted state of mind, and on another day gives a larger sum (such as one rupee) with that same structural state of mind, the underlying consciousness is formally the same type. However, because the volitional commitment in the second instance is greater and more pronounced, that state of consciousness is significantly more potent and yields far greater consequences.

---

#### 5. The Qualitative Hierarchy of Wholesome States
The decisive factor determining the ethical superiority of a wholesome state is the presence of **insight**. An act devoid of insight—regardless of its level of joy or spontaneity—can never equal or surpass an act guided by insight.

Among the secondary factors:
* A **joyful state** is superior to an emotionally neutral (equanimous) state.
* An **unprompted state** is superior to a prompted state.

Accordingly, the **joyful, insight-endowed, and unprompted state of consciousness** stands as the highest and most potent of all everyday wholesome states.

---

#### 6. The Phenomenological Reality, Common Misconceptions, and Practical Necessity
A frequent misconception regarding moral action is the belief that once a person undertakes an ethical endeavor—such as deciding to give a gift until its conclusion, or observing ethical vows from one morning to the next—the mind remains continuously and uninterruptedly occupied by wholesome consciousness throughout that entire duration.

In actual cognitive experience, consciousness operates in rapid, sequential mental processes. A wholesome state arises and ceases only during brief, active phases of moral volition, occurring in rapid sequences of seven consecutive moments.

During the broader timeframe of any ethical project, conflicting mental intrusions—such as pride, conceit, jealousy, or unrelated unwholesome thoughts—can easily intervene. When such unwholesome states interrupt the continuum, they weaken the power and purity of the wholesome act. Therefore, to ensure that a moral endeavor achieves its fullest potential, one must maintain continuous, vigilant mindfulness from the initial intention to its final completion, actively guarding the mind against the intrusion of greed, aversion, and delusion.
