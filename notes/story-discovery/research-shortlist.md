# Research Shortlist — First Story-Discovery Cycle

This file sets out 24 open human questions for the first cycle of documentary research. Each has a research brief. A later research agent takes **one** question at a time and looks for real, verifiable situations in Sri Lanka, following [research-agent-instruction.md](research-agent-instruction.md).

**Nothing in this file is a story.** No person, community, place or event has been identified, and none is implied. Every example is a *kind* of situation to look for. The questions are starting points, and reality is allowed to answer them in ways the philosophy did not expect.

## Selection method

1. **All 148 accepted cards were scored** in [documentary-potential-matrix.md](documentary-potential-matrix.md). 29 reached Tier A.
2. **Overlaps were resolved first** using [overlap-map.md](overlap-map.md). Where cards overlap, only the lead was eligible, and supporting IDs travel with it.
3. **Tier A leads and distinct ideas were the candidate pool.** No Tier C or Do-not-advance card was considered. Tier B cards were held in reserve.
4. **Territory balance was enforced.** Every one of the 14 territories has at least one question and none has more than three. Larger territories did not get more places because they have more cards.
5. **Within each territory, the choice favoured** openness to discovery, a place Chathura could travel into, something observable or filmable, relevance across Sri Lankan life, ethical safety, and room for reality to contradict the starting point.
6. **Distinctiveness across the list was checked.** Where two Tier A cards would lead research to the same situations, only one was kept (for example C052-I03 over C039-I01, and C058-I02 over C033-I01).
7. **Cards were not chosen for philosophical depth** or because an example in the card sounds cinematic. Card examples are hypothetical research directions, not verified stories.

Five Tier A cards were not shortlisted: C003-I02, C020-I01, C021-I02, C033-I01 and C039-I01. They remain strong candidates for a second cycle. Their reasons are at the end of this file.

## Rules that apply to every question

### Baseline evidence every story lead needs

Before any lead can move toward story selection, research must establish all of the following. Each brief then adds evidence specific to its question.

- **A real person or community** who agrees to take part, identified through verifiable means.
- **A current or recoverable situation**: something happening now, or a past situation that can still be documented through people, places, records or objects.
- **The person's own account**, in their own words and on their own terms.
- **Verifiable facts** where history, dates, places or events are involved, each with a source.
- **A meaningful place** that Chathura can travel into, where the situation exists.
- **Something observable or filmable** that is not only an interview.
- **Ethical access and informed participation**, including the right to withdraw.
- **A reason the story matters independently of the philosophy.** If every reference to Abhidhamma were removed, the story would still be worth telling.

### Global cautions

- The philosophy must stay underneath. No participant is told, or shown, that they illustrate a teaching.
- Never infer someone's inner state, motive or character from behaviour, occupation, appearance, community or circumstances.
- Never use karma, past action or rebirth to explain disability, poverty, illness, social position, appearance or any life circumstance.
- Never type people by temperament, or call anyone greedy, envious, hateful, proud, deluded or shameless.
- Doctrinal accounts of mind-moments, bodily elements or heart-base physiology are not science.
- Death, grief, illness, disability, addiction, suicide and self-harm need a safeguarding protocol and informed consent before any contact.
- Do not approach vulnerable people because their situation is dramatic.
- Participants may understand their experience in a way that contradicts the philosophical starting point. Their interpretation is recorded as theirs and respected.

## SQ01 — What goes unseen in a familiar place

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C009-I01 — *Most of what meets the eye goes unseen*
- **Supporting:** C040-I01 (includes merged alias C045-I01)
- **Related:** C013-I01, C036-I02
- **Working title:** Most of what meets the eye goes unseen
- **Primary territory:** T01 Noticing and overlooking

### Open human question
What in a familiar place have we never actually seen?

### Why it may produce a documentary
The tension is between confidence that we know a place and the discovery that we have been looking past most of it. Someone whose work or interest trains them to notice what others walk by can reveal a place's hidden layer, and a journey with them lets the audience see a known landscape change. It needs no explanation of attention: the film can simply look where that person looks.

### Story signals
- Someone who notices marks, species, sounds or changes that neighbours say they have never seen.
- A place that residents describe as unchanged while evidence shows slow change.
- A person returning to a childhood place and describing it differently from those who stayed.
- Two people walking the same route and reporting very different things.
- A small detail that turns out to carry a large history.

### Possible research domains
Everyday places, occupations, traditional knowledge, nature and landscape, historical memory, childhood, change, journeys, objects.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- What exactly the person notices, verified where possible by independent sources (such as records, experts or photographs).
- How long the thing noticed has been there or been changing.
- How the person learned to notice it, in their own words.
- Whether other people in the place recognise it once shown.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that noticing comes from interest or desire. It may come from training, tools, necessity or chance.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source's account of eye-sensitivity and the heart-base is traditional doctrine.
- **Scientific equivalence:** this is not a claim about visual attention research.
- **Moral labels:** not noticing is not carelessness or a failing.
- **Vulnerable groups:** children and people with visual impairments must not be used to illustrate "not seeing".
- **Alternative explanations:** poor visibility, unfamiliarity, fatigue, or simply not knowing what to look for (C040-I01 adds prominence: size, light and nearness).

### First research questions
1. Which places do people say they know completely, and who in those places sees them differently?
2. What do people who work outdoors, repair things or track living things report noticing that others do not?
3. How did those people learn to notice it, and from whom?
4. What has changed in the place over the years, and who noticed first?
5. When a detail is pointed out, do other residents recognise it, dispute it or find it meaningless?
6. What does the person themselves think they still fail to see?

## SQ02 — What long looking reveals

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C013-I01 — *Looking long enough for something to become clear*
- **Supporting:** C101-I02, C065-I01, C094-I02
- **Related:** C094-I01, C076-I02
- **Working title:** Looking long enough for something to become clear
- **Primary territory:** T01 Noticing and overlooking

### Open human question
What do we only notice once we have stopped looking for something else?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Skilled work that depends on long, returning attention to one object, sound or texture carries a quiet tension: the pull of everything else against the slow reward of staying. It can be filmed through hands, tools and time. Chathura can sit beside the practitioner long enough for the audience to start seeing what they see.

### Story signals
- A practice where the same object is examined repeatedly over hours, days or seasons.
- A practitioner who says what a newcomer misses and can show it.
- A craft or skill whose practitioners are becoming fewer.
- Different senses in use: sight, listening (C101-I02), touch (C065-I01) or one fixed working point (C094-I02).
- A mistake that taught the person to look longer.

### Possible research domains
Occupations, crafts, restoration, traditional medicine, agriculture, music, fieldwork in nature, ageing practitioners, apprenticeship, objects.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The practice itself, observed directly and over enough time to see what long attention involves.
- What the practitioner detects, checked where possible against an outcome or independent judgement.
- Whether the knowledge is being passed on, and to whom.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that the person is patient by nature or spiritually inclined.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source's definition concerns meditative absorption; absorption lasting a day is doctrinal.
- **Scientific equivalence:** no claim about expertise or perception science.
- **Moral labels:** fast or broad attention is not inferior; some discoveries come from wandering attention.
- **Vulnerable groups:** elderly practitioners must not be framed as "the last" without evidence.
- **Alternative explanations:** clarity may come from accumulated knowledge, training, instruments or context rather than sustained attention itself. For touch, expectation can mislead (C065-I01).

### First research questions
1. Which practices in Sri Lanka still depend on long, close examination by eye, ear or hand?
2. How do practitioners describe the difference between what they see at first and what they see after long attention?
3. What do apprentices or newcomers consistently miss?
4. What tools, conditions or times of day help or hinder the work?
5. Has the practice changed with new technology, and what was gained or lost?
6. How do practitioners know when they have looked long enough?

## SQ03 — One place, many sides

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C020-I02 — *Seeing only one side of something at once* (includes merged alias C065-I02)
- **Supporting:** none required
- **Related:** C046-I01, C036-I01, C052-I01
- **Working title:** Seeing only one side of something at once
- **Primary territory:** T02 What we take things to be

### Open human question
How much of a disagreement is about the thing, and how much is about which side of it each person stands on?

### Why it may produce a documentary
A river, forest, beach, building or road means different things to people who use it differently. Each may see accurately and still see something else. Travelling between those positions lets the audience experience the thing change shape, and the disagreement becomes a journey rather than a debate.

### Story signals
- A shared place or resource valued in openly different ways by different groups.
- A decision pending about that place, such as development, protection or reuse.
- A house or building understood differently by those who left and those who moved in.
- People who have tried to understand another group's view of the same place.
- Positions that do not follow expected lines.

### Possible research domains
Landscape, community, work, traditions, change, development, migration, historical memory, relationships, objects.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The factual situation of the place: ownership, use, history and any pending decision, from documents.
- At least three distinct, first-hand perspectives, each given on its own terms.
- Evidence that each perspective is grounded in real use or relationship, not assigned by the researcher.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that any party is selfish, sentimental or obstructive.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source describes a mental factor settling on one aspect of an object; it is not a theory of social viewpoint.
- **Scientific equivalence:** not a claim about perspective-taking research.
- **Moral labels:** the film must not rank perspectives as more or less enlightened.
- **Vulnerable groups:** communities facing displacement or livelihood loss need particular care; power imbalances must be named.
- **Alternative explanations:** differences may come from interests, power, legal rights or misinformation, not viewpoint alone. The source does not say all views are equally true.

### First research questions
1. Which places or resources are currently valued in openly different ways by the people who use them?
2. What does each group actually do in or with the place?
3. What does each person think the others do not see about it?
4. What decisions about the place are pending, and who makes them?
5. Has anyone moved between positions, such as a fisher who became a guide?
6. What facts about the place are disputed, and what records exist?

## SQ04 — Waiting on what will not settle

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C020-I03 — *Restless yet still gripping the same thing*
- **Supporting:** none required
- **Related:** C033-I01, C048-I01, C082-I01
- **Working title:** Restless yet still gripping the same thing
- **Primary territory:** T03 Preoccupation and old pulls

### Open human question
What keeps us holding on to the very thing that will not let us settle?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Waiting on an outcome, such as results, a hearing, a visa, a boat's return or news from abroad, creates a real period with a start and an end. People cannot put the matter down and cannot think it through calmly. Filming during the waiting, rather than after, leaves the outcome genuinely open and lets reality decide how the story ends.

### Story signals
- A known date when something will be decided or learned.
- Daily routines reshaped by waiting, such as checking a phone, visiting an office or watching the sea.
- Several people waiting on the same outcome in different ways.
- Places where people wait together.
- People who have waited like this before and describe what they learned.

### Possible research domains
Family, migration, work, the sea and fishing, education, law and bureaucracy, health, expectations, uncertainty, everyday behaviour.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The nature of the pending outcome and its timeline, verified.
- Consent that covers the possibility of a bad outcome being filmed or not filmed.
- A plan for what happens if the outcome is harmful or distressing.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that returning to the matter is agitation; it may be practical problem-solving, vigilance or love.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source describes a momentary unwholesome mind-state.
- **Scientific equivalence:** this is not rumination, anxiety or any clinical condition.
- **Moral labels:** worry is not a moral fault.
- **Vulnerable groups:** people awaiting medical results, court verdicts or news of missing relatives need safeguarding. People with legal cases must not be exposed to legal risk.
- **Alternative explanations:** the preoccupation may be sustained by the situation itself (unanswered calls, legal process, debt) rather than by any inner grip.

### First research questions
1. What kinds of outcomes do people in Sri Lanka commonly wait on for weeks or months?
2. Where do people wait, and what do they do while waiting?
3. How do different members of a family or group wait for the same thing?
4. What do people say helps them set the matter aside, if anything?
5. What changes in daily life during the waiting?
6. How do people describe the waiting afterwards, once the outcome is known?

## SQ05 — Parting with something long held

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C067-I01 — *Giving up the wanting, not the thing*
- **Supporting:** C100-I01
- **Related:** C080-I02, C021-I02, C026-I01, C005-I02
- **Working title:** Giving up the wanting, not the thing
- **Primary territory:** T04 Wanting, having and enough

### Open human question
Card's question: *When we get rid of something we crave, what happens to the craving?*

**Research form (refined without changing meaning):** When people part with something they have been strongly attached to, or keep it, what happens to the attachment itself?

The refinement avoids assuming that the attachment is craving and allows for the case in which the thing stays and the attachment changes.

### Why it may produce a documentary
Deciding whether to keep or give up family land, a house, a boat, jewellery or a collection is concrete and often current. The object can be filmed before and after. The tension is whether removing the thing changes the attachment, or whether the attachment can change while the thing stays. C100-I01 adds the case where a grip loosens because the thing turns out not to be one's own.

### Story signals
- A decision about a significant possession that is pending or recent.
- Family members who feel differently about the same object.
- Something given away or sold that the person still visits, watches or talks about.
- Something kept that the person says no longer holds them.
- An object whose ownership or origin is being reconsidered.

### Possible research domains
Family, possessions, land, inheritance, migration, ageing, objects, traditions, change, work.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The object's history and ownership, from documents where possible.
- The practical circumstances of the decision (debt, relocation, inheritance, duty).
- Accounts from more than one person connected to the object.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that keeping means attachment or that giving away means freedom. People part with things because of debt or family duty.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source's two sentences concern defilements, not ordinary people; no method of release is given.
- **Scientific equivalence:** none.
- **Moral labels:** attachment to land or heirlooms is not a fault; this must not become "let go" advice.
- **Vulnerable groups:** people under financial pressure or in contested inheritance; ownership disputes must not be adjudicated on film (C100-I01).
- **Alternative explanations:** economic necessity, legal constraints, family pressure, grief for a person rather than a thing.

### First research questions
1. What kinds of possessions do Sri Lankan families find hardest to sell, divide or give away?
2. Who in a family decides, and how do others experience that decision?
3. What do people do after parting with something, and what do they say they miss?
4. What have people kept that they say no longer matters to them, and why do they keep it?
5. How do customs of inheritance or dowry shape these decisions?
6. Has anyone discovered that something they held belonged to, or came from, someone else?

## SQ06 — Wants shaped by hardship

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C080-I01 — *When discomfort turns into wanting comfort*
- **Supporting:** C077-I03
- **Related:** C073-I01, C081-I01, C073-I02
- **Working title:** When discomfort turns into wanting comfort
- **Primary territory:** T04 Wanting, having and enough

### Open human question
How much of what we long for is shaped by the discomfort we once had to bear?

### Why it may produce a documentary
What someone buys, builds or protects first after a long hardship can reveal the shape of that hardship: a cool room after years of heat, a full rice store after hunger, a quiet day after heavy labour. Across generations, what felt like a treat changes (C077-I03). The question leads naturally into homes, kitchens and memories of lean years, and leaves room for people who say hardship made them want less.

### Story signals
- A household or community whose material conditions changed markedly within living memory.
- Something bought, built or kept that people link to an earlier lack.
- Elders and younger relatives describing treats or comforts differently.
- People who say they deliberately kept habits from hard times.
- Objects kept from lean years.

### Possible research domains
Family, ageing, food, work, migration, historical memory, change, possessions, everyday behaviour, community.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The hardship described, placed in verifiable historical context where possible.
- What changed and when, from more than one account.
- The person's own explanation of their wants, without prompting toward a link to hardship.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that spending on comfort is craving. Seeking relief is often necessity or care for family.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the feeling-to-craving link belongs to a doctrine about rebirth; it is not a psychological law.
- **Scientific equivalence:** none.
- **Moral labels:** comfort-seeking is not a failing; poor people are not "easily satisfied" (C077-I03).
- **Vulnerable groups:** people currently in hardship must not be filmed as a "before" image; poverty must not be romanticised.
- **Alternative explanations:** hardship may have made people more content, more generous or simply practical; wants may be shaped by advertising, status or opportunity.

### First research questions
1. What do people who lived through lean periods say they wanted most at the time?
2. What did they obtain first when conditions improved, and why that?
3. What do younger family members consider a treat, and how does that differ?
4. What habits from hard times have people kept or dropped?
5. Does anyone say hardship made them want less? What changed their mind or confirmed it?
6. What objects or foods carry memories of those times?

## SQ07 — Effort that answers another's sacrifice

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C076-I01 — *Wanting to be worth what others give up*
- **Supporting:** none required
- **Related:** C021-I03, C006-I03, C097-I02, C005-I02
- **Working title:** Wanting to be worth what others give up
- **Primary territory:** T05 Effort, readiness and the conditions for acting

### Open human question
What happens to our effort when we learn what someone else went without for us?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Many young people's education, training or migration is paid for by a family eating less, working longer or selling something. Knowing that cost can steady effort or weigh it down. The question naturally moves between the place of effort (a school, a workplace abroad, a training site) and the home where the sacrifice was made, giving the journey two ends.

### Story signals
- A person whose training or work was funded by a relative's visible sacrifice.
- The moment someone learned what that support had cost.
- Different responses among siblings supported in the same way.
- Letters, remittances, photographs or objects connected to the arrangement.
- People who say the knowledge helped them, and people who say it harmed them.

### Possible research domains
Family, education, migration, work, ambition, expectations, obligation, childhood, separation, community.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- Accounts from both the person supported and those who supported them, each given separately.
- The practical facts of the support (what was given up, for how long), verified where possible.
- Consent from family members who are discussed.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that effort comes from gratitude; it may come from fear of disappointing others, ambition or social expectation.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source concerns monastic alms given for merit; the elder's attainment is doctrinal.
- **Scientific equivalence:** none.
- **Moral labels:** obligation must not be presented as a virtue that excuses pressure; people who reacted with resentment or paralysis are not ungrateful.
- **Vulnerable groups:** minors, migrant workers in difficult conditions, family members in debt.
- **Alternative explanations:** effort may have been shaped by opportunity, teachers, peers or personal interest more than by sacrifice.

### First research questions
1. How do families in different parts of Sri Lanka fund education, training or migration?
2. When and how do the people supported learn what it cost?
3. How do they describe the effect of that knowledge on their effort, over time?
4. How do the people who gave something up describe what they expected in return, if anything?
5. What happens when the hoped-for result does not come?
6. What objects or places hold the memory of the arrangement?

## SQ08 — When to push and when to leave alone

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C076-I02 — *Knowing when to push and when to leave alone*
- **Supporting:** C087-I01 (includes merged alias C099-I01)
- **Related:** C013-I03 (includes merged alias C033-I02), C096-I01, C094-I01, C088-I03
- **Working title:** Knowing when to push and when to leave alone
- **Primary territory:** T05 Effort, readiness and the conditions for acting

### Open human question
How do we tell when something needs more effort from us and when it needs us to stop interfering?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Work that depends on timing, such as firing a kiln, tending a fire, coaching before a performance or nursing someone through recovery, makes this visible. Too much effort spoils the result, and so does too little. The hardest move may be recognising that things are going well and doing nothing. The rhythm of the work gives the film its structure.

### Story signals
- A process with stages that cannot be rushed, where intervention at the wrong moment causes failure.
- A practitioner who can describe a time they pushed too hard near the end (C087-I01).
- Teacher and learner disagreeing about when to act.
- Visible signs that the practitioner reads to decide.
- An outcome that is uncertain until the end.

### Possible research domains
Crafts, cooking, agriculture, sport, music, caregiving, teaching, recovery, weather, work.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The process observed across at least one full cycle.
- The signals the practitioner uses, described and shown.
- Examples of misjudgement, given voluntarily.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that restraint is wisdom; hesitating can also be avoidance.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the goldsmith and charioteer similes describe balancing mental factors toward liberation.
- **Scientific equivalence:** not a theory of performance or skill acquisition.
- **Moral labels:** people who push hard are not impatient by character.
- **Vulnerable groups:** people in medical recovery and their carers; athletes who are minors.
- **Alternative explanations:** timing may rest on tacit skill, instructions, instruments or luck; in many tasks pushing harder near the end is exactly right.

### First research questions
1. Which practices depend on alternating effort, rest and watching?
2. How do practitioners know when to add effort, ease off or do nothing?
3. What do beginners usually get wrong about timing?
4. When have practitioners pushed too hard close to the end, and what happened?
5. How is this judgement taught, if it can be taught?
6. What external signs (heat, colour, sound, behaviour) guide the decision?

## SQ09 — Ready in the first seconds

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C089-I01 — *Having a skill but not reaching it in time*
- **Supporting:** none required
- **Related:** C088-I03 (includes merged alias C099-I02), C087-I03
- **Working title:** Having a skill but not reaching it in time
- **Primary territory:** T06 Learning, mastery and judging progress

### Open human question
What turns something we know how to do into something we can do the moment someone needs it?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Knowing what to do in calm conditions differs from doing it at once when someone else is in danger. Lifeguards, fishing crews, midwives, rescue volunteers and drivers live with that gap. The work is physical, place-bound and high-stakes, and training and readiness can be observed without waiting for an emergency.

### Story signals
- Training that rehearses sudden situations repeatedly.
- Practitioners who describe the difference between their first real emergency and later ones.
- Teams whose readiness depends on each other.
- Places where danger arrives suddenly: coasts, rivers, roads, delivery rooms.
- Someone who once froze and later did not, telling it in their own words.

### Possible research domains
Occupations, the sea, roads, health care, community volunteers, training, fear, responsibility, teamwork, weather.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The role and training, verified through the organisation or community involved.
- Accounts of real incidents, checked against records where they exist, without exposing third parties.
- Permission from any institution involved.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that readiness reflects courage or depth of mastery.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source's ladder concerns supernormal powers; only its graded structure is used as analogy.
- **Scientific equivalence:** not a claim about stress response science.
- **Moral labels:** freezing is a normal stress response, not a character failure.
- **Vulnerable groups:** people rescued or treated, victims and families of incidents that ended badly.
- **Alternative explanations:** speed may depend on rehearsal of specific scenarios, fitness, equipment, teamwork or temperament.

### First research questions
1. Which roles in Sri Lanka require instant response on behalf of others?
2. How are people trained to act in the first seconds, and how often do they rehearse?
3. How do practitioners describe the gap between knowing and doing under pressure?
4. What do they say changed between their first and later emergencies?
5. What conditions (equipment, team, place) make readiness easier or harder?
6. How do they carry incidents that did not end well?

## SQ10 — Trusting what you have not experienced

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C073-I03 — *What a description cannot give you*
- **Supporting:** C093-I01
- **Related:** C020-I01, C091-I01, C094-I03
- **Working title:** What a description cannot give you
- **Primary territory:** T06 Learning, mastery and judging progress

### Open human question
How do we decide to trust an account of something we have never experienced?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Reading the sea, sensing weather, a craft's final touch or surviving a disaster are known by experience and passed on in words that cannot carry everything. A learner must decide whom to trust and what small experience of their own can anchor the rest. Chathura can be a real learner here, which lets the audience feel the gap between being told and knowing.

### Story signals
- Knowledge passed between generations mainly by speech and demonstration.
- A learner at the point of first trying what they were told.
- Recited steps, chants or cues used in teaching and later dropped (C093-I01).
- Disagreement between experienced people about what a sign means.
- A moment when experience contradicted the description.

### Possible research domains
Traditional knowledge, the sea, weather, agriculture, crafts, music, apprenticeship, disaster memory, ageing, language.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The knowledge itself, documented as practised and not only as described.
- How it is taught, observed directly.
- Independent context where claims about nature, weather or safety are involved.

### What must not be assumed

- **Scientific equivalence:** not a claim about memory science.
- **Moral labels:** nostalgia is not a fault.
- **Vulnerable groups:** people who left a place through displacement, conflict or disaster; returns can reopen trauma.
- **Alternative explanations:** memory strands may be inseparable in experience; people may reasonably value continuity in a place.

### First research questions
1. Who is planning, or has recently made, a return to a place significant to them?
2. What do they expect to find, and what do they remember most?
3. How has the place changed, according to records and current residents?
4. After returning, what do they say they had been missing?
5. How do current residents see the returning person?
6. What do photographs or objects from the earlier time show that memory does not?

## SQ23 — When shared plenty becomes divided

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C052-I01 — *When stockpiling turns shared plenty into boundaries*
- **Supporting:** C026-I01, C006-I02
- **Related:** C052-I02, C020-I02
- **Working title:** When stockpiling turns shared plenty into boundaries
- **Primary territory:** T14 Trust, talk and fairness in shared life

### Open human question
Card's question: *When does saving for tomorrow start to change how we treat what we once shared?*

**Research form (refined without changing meaning):** When a shared resource comes to be stored, divided or fenced, does it change how people treat it and each other, and if so, when?

The refinement avoids assuming that the change always happens.

### Why it may produce a documentary
Village tanks, grazing land, fishing grounds and inherited paddy fields are landscapes whose use has been shared, allocated and disputed over generations. The source's myth gives a sequence of storing, then scarcity, then boundaries, then taking. Reality will be more complex, involving population, policy and law. The land and water themselves show the history, and people who remember earlier arrangements can guide the journey.

### Story signals
- A common resource whose rules of use changed within living memory.
- Physical boundaries, gates, channels or markers added over time.
- People who remember earlier sharing.
- Beliefs about who is entitled to take what (C006-I02).
- Resistance to others' access (C026-I01), or new forms of sharing.

### Possible research domains
Land, water, agriculture, fishing, community, law, history, change, scarcity, traditions.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The documented history of the resource's ownership and management.
- Accounts from users with different positions and rights.
- The role of state policy, population and markets, from verifiable sources.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that storing is greed or that boundaries reflect distrust.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source is a myth with magical elements presented as cosmic history; it does not show that stockpiling causes theft.
- **Scientific equivalence:** not an economic theory of the commons.
- **Moral labels:** no community's norms or customary rights may be presented as moral failure.
- **Vulnerable groups:** users who lost access; parties in active disputes.
- **Alternative explanations:** division often follows population growth, state policy or real scarcity; boundaries can reduce conflict; storing is often a sensible answer to uncertainty.

### First research questions
1. Which shared resources have changed from common use to allocation within living memory?
2. What do older users remember about earlier arrangements?
3. What caused the change, according to records and different users?
4. Who gained and who lost access?
5. How are disputes about the resource settled now?
6. Are there new forms of sharing, and how did they start?

## SQ24 — Timing an unwelcome truth

### Idea identity
- **Lead idea:** C091-I03 — *Knowing when to say a true but unwelcome thing*
- **Supporting:** none required
- **Related:** C098-I01, C058-I03, C094-I03
- **Working title:** Knowing when to say a true but unwelcome thing
- **Primary territory:** T14 Trust, talk and fairness in shared life

### Open human question
How do we tell the difference between waiting for the right moment and avoiding a hard truth?

### Why it may produce a documentary
Doctors giving a diagnosis, elders settling family disputes, and inspectors reporting unsafe work regularly decide when a true, unwelcome thing can be heard. Waiting too long becomes avoidance; speaking at once may mean it is not heard. The tension is between honesty and care. Silence can also come from fear (C098-I01) or fear of being wrong (C058-I03), so research must keep those possibilities open.

### Story signals
- A role that regularly involves delivering unwelcome news or judgements.
- A practitioner who can describe getting the timing wrong.
- A recipient who can describe how the timing affected them.
- Customs or protocols for breaking bad news.
- A situation where delay caused harm, or haste did.

### Possible research domains
Health care, family, community mediation, work safety, teaching, religion as lived practice, relationships, responsibility, fear, ageing.

### What future research must establish
The baseline evidence, plus:
- The role and its context, verified.
- Accounts from both people who deliver and people who receive such news, with strict confidentiality.
- Institutional permission where relevant; no private medical or legal information disclosed.

### What must not be assumed
- **Inner motives:** that delay is care, or that bluntness is honesty.
- **Doctrinal claims:** the source describes the Buddha's speech as an object of recollection and gives no method for judging timing.
- **Scientific equivalence:** not a communication protocol.
- **Moral labels:** careful timing is not manipulation, and quick speech is not cruelty.
- **Vulnerable groups:** patients and families receiving serious news; people in family disputes.
- **Alternative explanations:** some situations, such as safety risks, allow no delay; silence may come from uncertainty, legal constraint or danger in speaking.

### First research questions
1. Which roles regularly involve deciding when to tell someone something unwelcome?
2. How do practitioners judge whether a person is ready to hear it?
3. What have they seen happen when timing was wrong in either direction?
4. How do recipients describe the effect of when and how they were told?
5. What customs or protocols guide these decisions?
6. When has waiting become avoidance, in practitioners' own view?
